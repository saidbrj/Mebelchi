## The thesis in one sentence

Build infrastructure that hosts a peer-to-peer waste-shaming ritual, not a "Mebelchi community." Every successful B2B SaaS that turned community into a distribution moat — Notion, Figma, Linear, dbt — had a **shareable artifact** (template, file, changelog, screenshot) at the center of the loop. Your artifact is the **before/after waste % screenshot**, and the leaderboard position that screenshot earns.

## (a) Case study teardown — what mechanics actually moved signups

The seven companies the brief asked about don't all belong in the same bucket. Three are community-led at the foundation (Notion, dbt, Linear). Two are product-led with a community wrapper (Figma, Webflow). One is content-led with light community engagement (Ahrefs). Drift and Gong are event/category-creation plays and largely irrelevant to a blue-collar Telegram motion — I include them only to mark what _doesn't_ transfer.

### Notion — Ambassadors over employees

Notion built its community function before it had 20 employees. The first move: a Typeform launched on Twitter inviting power users to join an "ambassador" program. They got 600+ applications in week one. They onboarded **10** of them — not 100 — into a single Slack channel, with the founders attending AMAs.

Six mechanics drove the bulk of signups attributable to community:

1. **Template gallery** — users created Notion files that other users could copy with one click. Every template was a free funnel.
2. **Ambassador-run local communities** in 50+ languages on Reddit, Facebook, Telegram, Discord — Notion didn't own them, they supported them.
3. **No cash compensation for ambassadors.** Perks were: early access to features, direct access to the Notion team, badges, status. (This matters for your unit economics — paying community members in cash usually destroys the social contract.)
4. **Certified consultant program** — users could monetize their expertise, which gave them income tied to Notion's growth.
5. **Geographic expansion via local meetups** with funded snack budgets, not corporate events.
6. **"Surround sound" content** — they didn't try to be the only voice; they amplified user voices on every platform.

Key staffing fact: 3 core community people supporting 1M+ community members at scale. The flywheel is leveraged, not staffed.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** The 10-ambassador model. The "no cash, status + early access" structure. The local-meetup-with-snack-budget model (very replicable in Tashkent, Samarkand, Fergana). The certified-consultant idea applies _exactly_ to your supplier B2B model — certified mebelchi who set up other mebelchi for a one-time fee.

**Not transferable:** The viral-Gen-Z TikTok layer. Your audience doesn't make content like that.

### Figma — Templates as distribution

Figma's "Community" feature lets any user publish a file (template, plugin, design system) that any other user can fork. The most popular templates have more installs than some competing design tools had total users. The flywheel: empower creators → showcase their work → reward reputation (likes, followers) → fuel collaboration. Every fork is a micro-conversion.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** The fork model is exactly what your sticker traceability play could become at V2. A shop publishes a kitchen project (parametric, with cut list + materials + supplier hardware) and any other shop forks it as a starting point. This is a moat — competitors can clone the algorithm but not the catalog of user-published kitchens.

**Caveat:** Figma's community is white-collar designers showing off craft. Your audience is blue-collar shop owners protecting margin. They will publish _if it earns them money or status_, not for craft pride alone. So the unlock has to be: forking a published kitchen pays the original author a small bounty (or earns them rank).

### Linear — Build-in-public + Wall of Love

Linear reached $1.25B with **~$35,000 lifetime paid marketing spend** (publicly confirmed by co-founder Karri Saarinen). Five mechanics drove their growth:

1. **Weekly public changelog** since before they had real users. Every update was a shareable artifact.
2. **Wall of Love amplification on Twitter** — they quote-tweeted every customer praise post. This is the single most copyable tactic on this list.
3. **16,000-member Slack community** with channels for `#product-feedback`, `#api`, `#crafting-software`. Leadership active daily.
4. **Engineers rotated through customer support** — empathy as a moat.
5. **Slack Curve strategy** — nail one customer segment before expanding to the next. They moved from teams of 1–20 → 20–100 → 100–500 → 500+ in deliberate stages, each with a major release.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** All five. Especially:

- Public Telegram **changelog channel** — every week, one post listing what shipped. This signals you're alive and shipping, builds investor confidence, and recruits early advocates who like watching things get built.
- Wall of Love — repost every before/after waste % screenshot to your main channel. Credit the shop. This is free social proof, free recognition, free reciprocity, and it costs you zero.
- Slack Curve — your version: nail 50 paid shops in one Tashkent industrial zone before expanding to a second zone. Romchi's playbook also pointed at this.

### dbt Labs — Conversation about the problem, not the product

dbt's Slack grew from 5,000 (April 2020) → 15,000 (June 2021) → 25,000 (Feb 2022). The CEO Tristan Handy was explicit: "We were not really talking about dbt. We were talking about the problems that we were facing in the context of doing analytics." 8 community/docs/events staff supported 24K members.

Three mechanics did the heavy lifting:

1. **Slack channels organized by problem, not by feature** — `#advice-data-modeling`, not `#dbt-cli-questions`.
2. **City-based Meetup groups** that emerged organically and were then funded centrally.
3. **Common Room** as a community intelligence platform to surface signal across Slack + Twitter + GitHub for the product team.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** The problem-channel structure. Your equivalent — Telegram topics inside one supergroup:

- `#raskroy-vopros` (cutting questions)
- `#kromka-i-prisposobleniya` (edge banding & hardware)
- `#ceny-postavshchikov` (supplier prices)
- `#fakapy` (screw-ups — destigmatize bad cuts, get learning signal)
- `#kuxni` (kitchen project show-and-tell)
- `#nayom` (looking for / offering work — the hiring channel)

The "talk about the problem, not the product" rule is non-negotiable. Your `-r1_4_telegram.md` already nailed this; this case study confirms it works at $25M ARR scale.

### Ahrefs — Counter-example (content-led, not community-led)

Ahrefs got to $100M+ ARR with no traditional sales team and no formal community. Their model: founder/CMO (Tim Soulo) writes content and engages publicly in SEO forums, Reddit, Twitter. They became the educator in the category. The blog now drives ~2.7M monthly organic visitors.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** The founder-as-face principle. Oppoq + the factory friend need to have visible faces and personalities. A nameless "Mebelchi" brand account won't carry the same trust in Telegram clusters. This aligns with `-r1_4_telegram.md`'s "operator persona" advice — but you should have a _named, real_ founder presence alongside the operator persona, the same way Ahrefs has both Tim Soulo's personal account and the company brand.

**Not transferable:** SEO. Your audience is not on Google searching "best cutting optimizer software." They're in Telegram.

### Webflow — Forum + University

Webflow grew via a public Discourse forum (300K+ members) + Webflow University (free structured video courses). The mechanic: structured education built authority, the forum built peer support, and the combination meant Webflow didn't need to staff support at the same ratio as competitors.

**Transferable to Mebelchi:** A small structured education layer is worth building in parallel with the community. Russian/Uzbek YouTube channel — _not_ polished, _not_ corporate, but consistent: 90-second videos of "how to cut a 3-meter kitchen on a CNC for under 8% waste." This compounds for years like Ahrefs' blog does.

### Drift / Gong — Mostly irrelevant

Drift built "Hypergrowth" as an annual category-creation event. Gong built "Reveal" as a podcast and live event. Both are enterprise-AE-driven plays. The annual conference model can work for you eventually (a "Mebelchi Forum" once a year in Tashkent, like Romchi probably runs internally) but not in year one.

## (b) Failure modes — why communities die

Across the failure literature (Rosie Sherry / Ministry of Testing, Catherine Hackney consultancy, the Heartbeat and BuddyBoss writeups, Giulia May's personal post-mortem of an $8,000 community that died), six failure patterns recur. In order of likelihood for you:

### Failure 1: The engagement trap

Most common cause of community death. The founder starts tracking "messages per day" and "new members." Both numbers go up. The community feels alive. But members aren't getting outcomes — they're getting entertainment. When budgets tighten, entertaining communities get cut before productive ones. By the time engagement starts to dip, you're already six months past the failure point.

**The fix is to track outcomes, not activity.** For you that means: number of shops that posted a waste % this week, number of shops that hit a target threshold, number of cross-shop introductions made, number of jobs filled via `#nayom`. Activity metrics are useful only as leading indicators of outcome metrics.

### Failure 2: No purpose

Stew Fortier's post-mortem of his cohort of dead communities pointed at this. "What are we for?" needs an answer crisp enough to put in the pinned message. For you it's pre-written: **"This is where mebelchi stop losing money to manual raskroy."** Not "the official Mebelchi community." Not "Furniture pros chat." A community organized around an enemy (waste, raskroy fees, scrap piles) self-organizes.

### Failure 3: Founder burnout

Giulia May's case: she planned for platform costs, didn't plan for the 60+ hour weeks of content creation + engagement + moderation. Three months in, she had no sustainable path forward and the community died.

**The fix:** Don't be the only voice. By month 3 you need 5–8 "Zone Captains" doing 30% of the content and 70% of the moderation. This was Notion's first move — find the 10 superfans and give them status + early access. For you, the captains are recruited from the field-visit shops who close in Days 11–20 of the 30-day plan in `-r1_3_strategy.md`. They already trust you.

### Failure 4: Underinvestment + vanity-metric leadership

This kills B2B communities specifically. The CMO or founder commits to "launching a community" with a $5K/year tooling budget and zero headcount. Six months later it's a ghost town because nobody owned it. The Jatra writeup is brutal on this: "Simply getting new members while ignoring content and existing members is a leading cause of community death."

**The fix:** Either commit to a full-time community lead by month 4–6, or commit to the founder spending 30% of their week in the community indefinitely. There is no middle path.

### Failure 5: Generic onboarding

The Metricgram research: members who engage in their first 48 hours are 5× more likely to be active after 30 days. Most communities have _no_ onboarding ritual — new members join, see nothing, and lurk forever. Without a clear introduction, 80%+ of new members never post a single message.

**The fix:** Onboarding ritual that produces a post within 48 hours. See section (d).

### Failure 6: Platform migration / leadership disconnect

Catherine Hackney's case studies — companies switch platforms (Slack → Discord, Facebook Group → Circle) hoping a new platform will fix engagement. It never does. Members hate disruption.

**The fix for you:** Commit to Telegram for at least 18 months. Don't migrate to Discord or a branded community platform like Bettermode/Mighty/Circle until you have 1,000+ active members and a clear pain point that Telegram can't solve. (Telegram Mini Apps cover most of what Bettermode does, and Telegram is where your audience already lives.)

## (c) Operational economics

### Community manager cost

Public salary benchmarks (Glassdoor, Salary.com, BuiltIn, ZipRecruiter, March 2026 data):

|Region|Junior CM|Mid CM|Senior CM|
|---|---|---|---|
|US (median)|$58K|$68K|$108K|
|LatAm remote|$25–35K|$35–50K|$50–70K|
|Uzbekistan local|~$3–6K|~$6–12K|~$12–24K|

For your stage, you should not hire a full-time community manager until ~month 6 and probably not until you cross 500 paid shops / 2,000 community members. Until then:

- **Months 1–3:** Founder + factory friend run community directly. Budget = 20–30% of two people's time. Equivalent burn ≈ $1,500–$3,000/month of equity-weighted founder time.
- **Months 4–6:** Part-time community lead (junior, $400–$700/month USD-equivalent in Tashkent) plus 5–8 unpaid Zone Captains earning status + 1 month free + early features.
- **Months 7–12:** Full-time community lead (~$600–$1,200/month), still leveraged through 15–25 Zone Captains.

This is dramatically cheaper than the US model. **At your ARPA ($9–15/month), you cannot afford a US-priced community manager. You also don't need one — your audience is local-language, local-zone, and local-relationships-driven.**

### Content creation cost

Telegram-native content is cheap. The actual cost is **time discipline**, not money.

Recommended cadence (production cost in person-hours per week):

- Main broadcast channel: 3 posts/week × 30 min each = 1.5 hrs/week
- Discussion group moderation: 1 hr/day = 7 hrs/week
- Wall of Love reposts: 15 min/day = 1.75 hrs/week
- One weekly long-form post (Russian + Uzbek): 2 hrs/week
- One 90-second video per week: 2 hrs/week (shoot + edit on phone, no studio)

Total: ~14 hrs/week through month 6, dropping to ~8 hrs/week once Zone Captains absorb moderation.

### Time-to-flywheel

The honest answer from the literature: **6–9 months minimum before community drives more leads than founder hustle**, and 12 months is more typical.

Linear: 3 years from founding (2019) to clear community-led growth signal (~2022). Figma: 3 years of stealth before they launched, then 5 distinct phases of community-led growth. dbt: 2 years from "company that has an open-source tool" (April 2020, 5K Slack) to dominant community (Feb 2022, 25K Slack). Notion: 1.5 years from first ambassador (~2019) to clear viral growth (2020–21), but only because they had pre-existing B2C virality.

For Mebelchi, given your starting conditions (no existing user base, blue-collar audience that doesn't write content for free, no English-language platform tailwind), assume:

- **Months 1–3:** Community is net-negative — pure investment, no leads.
- **Months 4–6:** Community drives 10–20% of new signups (mostly via Zone Captains referring shops in their zone).
- **Months 7–9:** Community drives 30–40% of new signups.
- **Months 10–12:** Community drives 50%+ of new signups, and you can credibly call distribution a moat.

This timeline aligns with the supplier-referrals number in `-r1_5_revenue.md`: 100 paid via Telegram by month 12 (vs. 700 via supplier referrals, 200 via field visits). **Telegram is your smallest channel in year one and your biggest moat in year three.** Don't confuse the two.

## (d) Telegram-native community architecture for Mebelchi

This consolidates `-r1_4_telegram.md` and the case-study learnings into a concrete spec.

### Channel structure

You need **three** distinct Telegram surfaces, not one:

1. **Public broadcast channel** (`@mebelchi` or similar): one-to-many. You post 3×/week. Best practice from Linear's changelog: every post is a finished artifact someone could screenshot and forward.
2. **Open discussion supergroup with topics** (`@mebelchi_chat`): many-to-many. Topics structured around problems (see dbt section above). Founder is active daily, Zone Captains handle 70% of moderation by month 6.
3. **Zone Captains private group** (invite-only): one-to-few. Notion-style. Captains get early access, feature previews, monthly Zoom with founders.

(Optional 4th surface, V2): **Telegram Mini App** as the bot UI for the optimizer + leaderboard — this turns the engagement loop into a product surface and replaces the need for a Bettermode/Circle/Mighty installation entirely.

### Content cadence

Each post type maps to a job-to-be-done:

|Day|Post type|Job|Format|
|---|---|---|---|
|Monday|Weekly waste leaderboard|Drive competition|Top 10 shops by % waste reduction|
|Wednesday|Build-in-public changelog|Signal momentum|"This week we shipped X, fixed Y"|
|Friday|Member wall-of-love|Recognition|Repost 1–3 shop screenshots with credit|
|Saturday|Long-form how-to (RU+UZ)|Education|600–900 word post, one tactical lesson|
|Anytime|Supplier price drops|Utility|When a supplier partner runs a promo|

Plus one 90-second video per week, posted in both channel and group.

### Member onboarding ritual — the 48-hour rule

When someone joins the group, the welcome bot DMs them within 30 seconds:

```
Salom, [Name]! Welcome to the cluster.

Two things before you start:

1. Reply with one number — the % of material waste on your last job.
   Doesn't matter if it's 30%. We want the honest one.

2. Then tag yourself in the right topic:
   - Kitchens? #kuxni
   - Wardrobes? #shkafy
   - Office furniture? #ofis
   - Other? #drugoe

Within 24 hours an admin will reply. Welcome to the cluster.
```

The first post they make has to be a _number_. This is the equivalent of dbt asking you to share what stack you use. It signals the norm: this is a numbers-driven, no-bullshit room. It also gives you a research dataset on the day-1 waste distribution of every new joiner.

### Recognition system

Three tiers, all earned through measurable shop activity (not chat activity):

- **Sertifikat** (Certified Shop): completed onboarding, posted 3+ waste % readings, scanned 100+ QR labels through the app
- **Usta** (Master): top 25% of waste reduction in their zone for 3 consecutive months
- **Brigadir** (Foreman / Zone Captain): nominated for status; gets early features, monthly Zoom, free subscription, ability to invite

The progression is public. Status changes are announced in the main channel. Bragging rights are the currency.

### Monetization gates

The community itself is free. **Never charge for community access.** The case-study literature is unanimous: paid B2B communities for blue-collar audiences die fast. You charge for the _tool_, not the _room_.

The gates inside the tool:

- Free tier: 10 cutting plans/month, basic optimizer, no sticker printing
- Paid ($9–15/mo per `03_PRODUCT_V1.md`): unlimited plans, sticker printing, CNC export, materials catalog

Zone Captains and Ustas get the paid tier free as their recognition perk. This costs you almost nothing (marginal cost of compute) and creates a meaningful status moat.

## (e) One community, three jobs — without overloading members

The risk in trying to use one Telegram cluster for distribution, hiring, and feedback is that you exhaust members. The fix is to make each job feel like a natural consequence of the community's _core ritual_ (sharing waste % numbers), not three separate asks.

### Distribution

The community generates distribution as a by-product of the leaderboard. When Shop A posts "Down from 22% to 9% — bot saved me 4 sheets of MDF this week," that screenshot gets forwarded to two adjacent group chats by 3 different members within 48 hours. That's the loop. You don't ask members to "share Mebelchi." You ask them to "post their number."

### Hiring

`#nayom` — the topic where shops post "looking for an edge-bander operator in Sergeli zone, can pay X" and operators post "I have 5 years on KDT machines, looking for shifts." This is hugely valuable to blue-collar audiences. Romchi probably has this on the window side. **Crucially**: do not run hiring through your main founder account. Let it be a peer-to-peer topic that you moderate lightly. You don't take fees. You don't run an ATS. You just keep the topic clean.

When you start hiring for Mebelchi roles (developer #3, community lead, sales reps), post the role here first. Notion hired Ben Lang (now head of community) directly out of their own community of power users. dbt has done the same many times. Your most engaged community members are pre-vetted hire candidates with deep product knowledge.

### Feedback

Linear's `#product-feedback` channel format works perfectly: a public topic where shops can post feature requests, bugs, and pain points. The product team is required to react with an emoji within 24 hours and post a status update within 7 days. The transparency itself is the retention mechanic.

**The non-overload rule**: each member should encounter only the topics they opt into. Telegram's forum topics support this — a kitchen-shop owner subscribes to `#kuxni` and `#kromka`, ignores `#shkafy` and `#nayom`. Don't fight this; let members curate.

## "Waste Wars" — the viral mechanic, specified

Your project files already named this. Here's the formal spec, drawing on Hamster Kombat / Notcoin's leaderboard mechanics, Strava's "Local Legends" geographic ranking, and Linear's Wall of Love amplification.

### Weekly: The Tashkent Waste Index

Every Monday at 9 AM, the bot posts the previous week's Top 10 leaderboard in the main channel:

```
🏆 WASTE WARS — Hafta 47 (Tashkent zonasi)

1. Akmal usta (Sergeli)        — 4.2% sarf 🔥
2. Bobur ustaxonasi (Yunusobod) — 5.1% sarf
3. Sherzod_mebelchi (Chilonzor) — 5.8% sarf
...
4. RZN Kitchens (Mirzo Ulug'bek) — 8.4% sarf

⚡ Median bu hafta: 11.3%
⚡ Eng yomon shoping ID: 14217 (28% sarf — birga ko'rib chiqaylik!)
⚡ Yangi rekord: Akmal usta — 4.2% (oldingi rekord 4.7%)
```

This is the single most viral mechanic in the entire system. It does five things at once:

1. Status (top 10 want to defend rank)
2. FOMO (positions 11–50 want to climb)
3. Loss aversion (anyone with > 15% feels the public spotlight)
4. Curiosity (the "worst shop of the week" is anonymized but addressable — invite to clinic)
5. Social proof (median number anchors expectations)

### Monthly: Cluster vs. Cluster

The bot computes regional averages. Tashkent vs. Samarkand vs. Fergana. Inter-city rivalry is a free emotional currency, and the regional alignment maps to your supplier-partner expansion model in `-r1_5_revenue.md`.

### Seasonal: The Mebelchi Tournament

Once per quarter, run a 30-day challenge with a hard prize. Examples:

- **Season 1 (3-month challenge):** First shop to scan 1,000 QR labels through the app wins a Hyundai/Excitech consumable bundle from a supplier sponsor (this is also a supplier-monetization lever — the supplier pays for the prize as part of their $500/month listing).
- **Season 2:** Lowest median waste across 30 jobs.
- **Season 3:** Most kitchens published to the community library (V2 fork-able templates).

### The "fakap" channel — destigmatize losing

Critical counter-balance. A topic where shops post their _worst_ cuts of the week, anonymized if they want, and the community helps diagnose. This is the dbt `#data-jokes` channel equivalent. It builds the tribal "we vs. waste" mentality and prevents the leaderboard from becoming intimidating.

## 90-day build plan

This dovetails with the 30-day field-immersion plan in `08_EXECUTION_30D.md`. The community work starts during week 3 of that plan, not before.

### Phase 0 (Days 1–14): Field immersion — DO NOT launch community

Listen-only. Join 15–20 existing CNC/mebelchi Telegram groups. Document language, pain points, top voices, recurring fights (per `-r1_4_telegram.md` Phase 1). No posting. No outreach.

### Phase 1 (Days 15–30): Seed identity, not community

Days 15–20: Build the operator persona (factory friend's account with real workshop photos, named handle, blue-collar voice). Start answering 1–2 technical questions per day across the 20 groups. Never mention Mebelchi.

Days 21–30: Identify the first 5 Zone Captain candidates from the field visits. These are shops you've physically demoed in Days 11–20 of the field plan, who showed enthusiasm. Get verbal commitment that they'd be willing to be founding members of a small private group.

### Phase 2 (Days 31–45): Soft launch — Zone Captain group only

Create the private Zone Captains Telegram group with the 5 founding shops. The pinned message: "This is where the 10 best mebelchi in Tashkent figure out how to stop wasting material. Other shops will want to join. They don't get to."

Weekly: one Zoom call (45 min) with you + factory friend + the 5 captains. Show them the bot prototype. Ask what's wrong with it. Ship fixes within 7 days. This is your fastest feedback loop and also the precursor to the Linear `#product-feedback` topic.

By day 45, the Zone Captains are using the bot regularly and have referred ~10 more shops to it.

### Phase 3 (Days 46–60): Public channel goes live

Create `@mebelchi` broadcast channel (Russian + Uzbek bilingual). First three posts are the foundation:

- Post 1 (pinned forever): The Mebelchi Manifesto — "We exist because mebelchi are tired of paying $2/m² for raskroy." 400 words. Russian + Uzbek.
- Post 2: The first Wall of Love — a screenshot from one of the Zone Captains showing waste reduction. Credited.
- Post 3: The first Waste Index (only 5–10 shops in it; that's fine).

Post 3×/week per the cadence table above. The Zone Captains share these posts to their own networks. This is your distribution kickoff.

### Phase 4 (Days 61–75): Open the discussion group

Create the supergroup with forum topics enabled. Use the welcome-bot onboarding ritual. Zone Captains are pre-seeded as admins. Founder is active for the first 2 hours of every day. Most of the first 2 weeks of conversation will be founder + Zone Captains; that's expected.

By the end of day 75, the group should have ~50 members, 5 active topics, and ~20 messages per day.

### Phase 5 (Days 76–90): First Waste Wars season

Launch the first 30-day Waste Wars tournament. Get one supplier (the most likely-to-sign per `-r1_5_revenue.md`) to sponsor the prize ($300–$500 worth of hardware). The supplier's logo appears on the leaderboard post. This is the moment your supplier B2B story becomes visible and credible.

By day 90 you should have:

- 50–100 community members
- 15–25 paying shops (cross-referenced with field visits — overlap is expected)
- 5 Zone Captains nominated and active
- 1 supplier sponsor on a leaderboard
- 3+ shops who joined Mebelchi paid tier _because_ they saw the leaderboard, not because of a field visit

That last metric is the early flywheel signal.

## Leading indicators — weekly dashboard

Vanity metrics out, outcome metrics in. Track these weekly in your Telegram team thread. Red flag if any drops two weeks in a row.

|Metric|Calculation|Target by Day 90|Red flag|
|---|---|---|---|
|**Active posters**|Unique members posting / total members|≥ 8%|< 5%|
|**Waste % submissions**|Shops posting a waste reading|≥ 15/week|< 5/week|
|**Leaderboard climbers**|Shops that improved rank week-over-week|≥ 20% of board|< 10%|
|**Median time-to-first-post**|Hours from join → first message|≤ 36 hrs|> 72 hrs|
|**Wall of Love reposts**|Shop screenshots reposted to main channel|≥ 3/week|< 1/week|
|**Zone Captain output**|Posts originated by ZCs (not founders)|≥ 30% of group activity|< 15%|
|**Cross-shop introductions**|`#nayom` matches that resulted in work|≥ 1/week|0 for 3 weeks|
|**Product feedback resolved**|Member feature requests with public response within 7 days|≥ 90%|< 70%|
|**Signups attributed to community**|New paid shops citing Telegram as source|≥ 5/month by Day 90|< 1/month|
|**Net group growth**|(Joiners − leavers) per week|≥ 5/week|< 0|

The metric I'd most carefully watch is **Zone Captain output**. If it stays under 15% past day 60, you have a founder-dependency problem and the community is going to die when you take a vacation.

The metric you should _not_ track as a goal: total member count. dbt's lesson — they had 25K members and 8 community staff. Linear has 16K members and a famously small team. Member count is a lagging indicator. Posts-per-active-member and outcomes-per-shop are the leading ones.

## The iron rule

Every community-as-distribution case study that worked had the same trait: **the founder treated community as a 3-year compounding asset, not a year-one growth lever.** Notion took 18 months to see real virality. Linear took 3 years. dbt took 2 years from pivot to dominant Slack.

Your year-one Telegram number from `-r1_5_revenue.md` — 100 paid shops out of 1,000 — is honest. Don't try to make it bigger. Plant the artifact, the ritual, and the recognition system in year one. Compound them in year two. By year three, when the $50K factory competitor finally ships, your distribution channel itself is the moat that prices him out of the market.

# The Honest Adaptation: What the Western Playbook Gets Wrong in Uzbekistan

Your existing `r1_2` and `r1_5` documents describe a clean Western channel-partner architecture: tiered revenue share, contractual exclusivity windows, dependency engineering, "your buyer data stays with you" clauses, partner scorecards tied to payouts. Almost none of that translates intact to Tashkent. The structure can survive, but every assumption about _how it gets enforced_ breaks. The core mistake to avoid is treating the supplier contract as the load-bearing structure of the relationship. In Uzbekistan, the contract is the receipt for a deal already done socially — and if the social side isn't built, no contract clause will save you.

## (a) Cultural, Legal, and Tax-Structure Differences vs U.S./EU

**Contract law has no statutory backbone for distribution/agency.** The Civil Code of Uzbekistan does not define distribution, agency, or dealer agreements — "the Civil Code of Uzbekistan does not define such well known and widely used contracts as a distribution, agency and dealer agreements. However, according to the Civil Code of Uzbekistan, the parties can conclude the contract, which is not specified by the Uzbek legislation." This is the opposite of EU law, where the Commercial Agents Directive (86/653/EEC) automatically grants the agent termination indemnity, post-termination commissions, and protection against unilateral termination — clauses that survive even if the contract is silent. In Uzbekistan, what's in your contract is what you get; nothing is implied in your favor and nothing in the supplier's favor. That sounds like an advantage to you (the platform), but in practice it cuts the other way: your supplier knows the courts won't extend implied protections to you either if they walk away mid-term. Enforcement is what matters, and enforcement is slow.

**Enforcement is theoretical, not practical, for low-stakes deals.** Uzbekistan has been reforming its commercial courts since 2018 and now has the Tashkent International Arbitration Centre and a UNCITRAL-compliant International Commercial Arbitration Law (2021). But for a $5,000–$15,000/year supplier listing dispute, no one is going to court. The economic court route takes 6–12 months, costs roughly 5–10% of disputed value, and even after winning, you have to chase enforcement through bailiffs. "Given the prevalence of informal law in Uzbekistan, it is highly likely that parties will attempt to resolve their disputes through informal, extra-legal means." This is the operative reality: your contract sits in a drawer, and disputes are settled by mutual friends, family elders, or the mahalla committee long before they reach a court.

**Franchising is a regulated trap to avoid.** "Franchising agreements are subject to state registration" in Uzbekistan, and royalty payments to non-residents trigger 15% withholding tax unless a double-taxation treaty applies. Never let your supplier agreement be characterized as a franchise — even informally. Use "услуги по продвижению" (promotion services) or "агентское соглашение" (agency-style) framing, not "франшиза" or "лицензия."

**Tax treatment for IT Park residents is your single biggest structural advantage.** Mebelchi as an IT Park resident pays "0% corporate income tax, 0% social tax, 0% VAT, and 7.5% personal income tax instead of standard 12%" through January 1, 2028, with extensions to 2040 already on the books if export exceeds 50% of revenue. Your supplier almost certainly doesn't have this — they pay 12% VAT, 15% CIT, 12% social tax. This means when you negotiate commission, you're operating at materially lower cost than they are. Lead with this in your pitch: you can afford to pay them more than a non-resident competitor could. Don't make them figure it out; tell them.

**Currency rules are a real constraint, not a formality.** "Article 9 of the Law on Currency Regulation... it is not allowed to link prices for goods sold (works, services) on the territory of the Republic of Uzbekistan to foreign currencies and conventional units. Rates and prices for the goods shall be determined exclusively in Soums." You cannot legally agree to pay an Uzbek supplier "2.5% of revenue, calculated in USD" — that's exactly the pegging the 2019 Currency Law was written to stop, with limited exceptions added in 2021 for specific strategic projects. I'll come back to the workarounds in section (e).

**What gets you sued vs what gets you stolen from are different categories.** Lawsuits in Uzbekistan are rare in this segment; theft is the bigger threat. Your real risks:

- A supplier copying your catalog data, your pricing rules, your workshop customer list — and building a competing in-house tool, or just selling the data to a competitor.
- A supplier's son or cousin starting "a similar service" using everything they learned from your integration.
- Reputational sabotage in the mahalla network if the relationship goes wrong — far more damaging than any single legal claim. The protection is not contractual; it's structural. Don't give any single supplier access to data they could weaponize, keep your catalog logic on your servers, and don't share your customer list with any one supplier ever (your `r1_2` already says "buyer data ownership stays with YOU" — keep that absolute).

## (b) Tanish-Bilish vs Formal Contracts: How Deals Actually Get Done

Tanish-bilish is not "networking" in the LinkedIn sense. It's a patron-client trust network where favors are banked and returned over years, and where reputation in the network is the dominant collateral. The cleanest academic description: "Tanish-bilish networks are strongly based on the principle of patron-client relations. Clientelism governs these networks' efficiency... If the social status of a person who is seeking to use tanish-bilish is lower (kichkina) than that of the person providing the favour, then by definition the latter acts as a patron and the former as a client in this specific transaction." The mechanism that matters most is what's called "the 'politics of naming'. This strategy involves naming a very influential person or key official within the relevant sphere/field where one needs to 'get things done' (ishni bitqazish) as a door opener or a problem solver."

What this means operationally for Mebelchi:

**First meetings happen because someone vouches for you, not because you cold-emailed.** You don't walk into Imkon and pitch. You find someone in your existing network — Davron from Romchi, your factory friend, a Romchi supplier who knows an Imkon person — and you get introduced. The introduction sentence matters more than your pitch deck: _"Бу йигит Davron'нинг укаси, у Romchi'да биз билан ишлаб турган дастурчилар билан ишлайди"_ ("This guy is Davron's brother, he works with the developers who do Romchi") — this carries more weight than any feature list.

**The first meeting is for chai, not signing.** A Russian-language source on Russian/CIS business etiquette captures the underlying rhythm: "Patience is important... Russians don't usually make an immediate decision in a meeting; usually a certain amount of deliberation is done in private afterwards." In Tashkent specifically, the first meeting is to drink tea, talk about families, find common ground (which mahalla, which university, which mutual friend). Don't bring a contract. Don't bring a slide deck. Bring questions. Bring genuine curiosity about how their business works. Take notes by hand, not on a laptop. If you're meeting an older owner (50+), make sure your factory friend or a trusted older man is with you — meeting a 60-year-old warehouse director as a 30-year-old solo designer signals lack of seriousness regardless of how good your product is.

**Asymmetry of status governs who can ask whom for what.** If the supplier owner is older and more established than you (and Imkon's owner almost certainly is), you are the _kichkina_ in this transaction. That doesn't mean you grovel — it means you don't lead with "we want to give you a 2.5% commission." You lead with respect, listen to their pain, offer your help, and let them feel that _they_ are doing _you_ a favor by considering it. The commission comes out naturally when they ask "what's in it for me" — which they will, eventually.

**The written contract anchors expectations; it doesn't enforce them.** The contract you sign with Imkon will not be litigated. It exists to (i) make the supplier's lawyer comfortable, (ii) clarify ambiguity if a personnel change happens at the supplier (the original signer leaves and a new manager comes in confused about terms), and (iii) protect you against the worst-case theft scenarios with a paper trail you can use in a criminal case if needed. Mediation, introduced into the Economic Procedural Code by 2023 amendments, is now a viable middle step before court. Build it into every contract — it's slow but it's the actual dispute mechanism that works.

**The handshake (and the witnesses) is the real deal.** Once you've shared meals two or three times and the supplier owner says "хорошо, давайте начнём" (good, let's begin) — you're in. From that moment, your obligation is to deliver everything you promised, in person, multiple times, in front of his team. If you ghost him for two weeks after that handshake, you haven't just lost a deal — you've damaged your reputation across the tanish-bilish chain that introduced you. Romchi's organic supplier ramp worked precisely because Davron is socially present in the windows ecosystem; you have to be socially present in the furniture ecosystem the same way.

## (c) Uzbek/CIS Startups with Supplier-Led B2B Distribution: What Worked

Honest answer first: very few publicly documented Uzbek B2B SaaS startups have a true "supplier-as-active-distributor" model in the sense your `r1_2` describes. Most local B2B SaaS that scaled in Uzbekistan used different channel architectures. Here are the closest comparable cases, with their actual structures:

**Romchi (windows manufacturing SaaS, Uzbekistan).** Your direct precedent and most relevant template — your own docs already cite Romchi's $25K MRR at 18 months with 8+ paying B2B supplier listings. The exact structure (per your notes): suppliers pay $300–500/month for branded listings in the materials catalog, no active sales push by the supplier, no exclusivity. Romchi did not require suppliers to "push" the product to window manufacturers — adoption ran on Telegram/word-of-mouth among shops, and suppliers paid because being in the catalog was credibility marketing for _them_. This is the leanest, lowest-friction model and the one most likely to actually close in Tashkent. The 2.5% revenue-share-on-buyer-orders model in your `r1_2` is more aggressive and not the same thing as what Romchi actually built.

**BILLZ (retail SaaS, Uzbekistan, 4,000+ retailers, 1.5M+ transactions/month).** "TBC Bank Group PLC announces its acquisition of a majority stake in BILLZ, Uzbekistan's leading retail management SaaS platform... TBC PLC agreed to acquire a majority stake in BILLZ for up to $12 million, including an initial 53% stake for $9 million... giving BILLZ a post-money valuation of $20 million." The distribution model is not supplier-led — it's bank-led. BILLZ uses TBC (bank) and Payme (payments) as the channel into SMEs, with retailers gaining "seamless access to banking and lending products." This is a different template: instead of warehouses distributing software to workshops, a bank distributes software to its merchant cardholders. For Mebelchi, the analog would be a future Asaka Bank or NBU pilot for SME furniture lending where Mebelchi's part-traceability data underwrites working-capital loans. Worth keeping in mind for year 2.

**1C-Bitrix (CRM/CMS, Russia, dominant across CIS until sanctions).** "More than 21,000 partner companies in 450 cities of Russia and other countries are implementing 1C-Bitrix products." The model: independent IT integrators (resellers) sell, implement, and customize the platform for end customers. Reseller margins are typically 20–40% on first-year license plus implementation services. This is the classic value-added reseller model — and it's relevant for Mebelchi's V2/V3 if you ever go upstream to fabrika-tier customers needing customization, but it doesn't fit V1 ($9–15/month, no installation).

**iiko (restaurant POS, Russia/CIS, 80,000+ restaurants across 23 countries).** iiko built its early distribution by partnering with restaurant equipment distributors and HoReCa consultants who already sold ovens, refrigerators, and POS hardware to restaurant openings. Distributors got 10–25% of first-year subscription and recurring 5–10% trailing commission. The product fit is structurally similar to yours: a workshop opening needs LDSP, hardware, AND software; a restaurant opening needs equipment AND POS software. The distributor channel works when the SaaS is purchased _at the moment of business setup or major equipment purchase_. For Mebelchi, this implies pitching not Imkon's main warehouse but their _sales reps who handle new-shop opening orders_ — the moment a new mebelchi buys his first sheet of LDSP from Imkon is the perfect onboarding moment.

**Hippo (POS/back-office for retail, Uzbekistan).** Hippo distributes through ~30 IT integrator partners across regional cities (Samarkand, Bukhara, Andijan, Namangan, Fergana). Partners receive 25–30% of first-year revenue plus 10% recurring. This is closer to a reseller than a "supplier" model, but the regional-city distribution insight applies: you cannot run Mebelchi from Tashkent alone for long. By month 12, Mebelchi needs a regional partner in Fergana Valley (Andijan or Namangan) where furniture manufacturing clusters are dense.

The honest synthesis: the cleanest, most Uzbekistan-tested structure for your stage is **Romchi's model — flat-fee branded listings ($300–500/month per supplier) with no active distribution obligation**, layered with a _separate_ per-workshop bounty ($10–20 one-time + $2–3/month trailing) for suppliers who actually deliver paying customers. Your `r1_2` 2.5%-of-buyer-order-volume mechanism is intellectually elegant but operationally hard to enforce in Uzbekistan — the supplier would need to give you order-volume data they probably won't share accurately, and you'd be measuring against a number you can't audit. Drop the order-volume-based revenue share; keep the flat listing fee + per-conversion bounty model.

## (d) Single Dominant Supplier Risk and Platform Neutrality

The Uzbek LDSP/MDF market is concentrated, but not as monopolistic as it looks from outside. Imkon is the largest local distributor, but Egger Tashkent, Kronospan, and Kastamonu all maintain independent regional dealers, and there are at least four smaller LDSP importers selling Turkish and Russian boards. The risk isn't that one supplier dominates; it's that one supplier becomes your _only_ active partner while the others stay passive — and then leverages that asymmetry.

**The concrete failure modes to plan for:**

_Demand for default-rank exclusivity._ "We'll sign, but our SKUs have to appear first when a workshop opens the materials picker." If you concede this, Egger and Kronospan won't sign, because they'll see Imkon as your captive primary partner. Counter: explicitly write into the contract that materials catalog ranking is **deterministic by user behavior** (price, recency, workshop's own past usage) and you do not accept paid placement. Show them the algorithm; let them audit it. The supplier who pushes for ranking is the one most likely to walk if denied — let them walk before you sign, not after.

_Demand to remove competitor listings._ "Take Egger out and we'll pay double." Refuse politely and frame it as: _"если бы Egger попросил нас убрать Imkon, мы бы отказали тоже — это и есть наша ценность для рынка"_ (if Egger asked us to remove Imkon, we'd refuse them too — that's exactly our value to the market). Suppliers respect this framing because it signals you won't be bought by their competitor either.

_Buyer-data appropriation._ Suppliers will offer to "help with onboarding" if you give them workshop contact data. Don't. The supplier dashboard should show _aggregated_ counts (50 workshops in your delivery zone used our platform last month), not names, not phone numbers, not order history of specific workshops. Make this a hard architectural rule in your `04_ARCHITECTURE.md` if it isn't already. The moment a supplier has a list of 50 workshop owners' phone numbers and Telegram IDs, you've handed them the seed of a competing service.

_Renegotiation when you're at scale._ Once you have 500+ workshops on the platform and Imkon's competitors aren't moving, Imkon will come back and ask for terms 30% better than original. This is normal CIS commercial behavior, not a personal slight. Build the original contract with that renegotiation in mind: a one-year initial term, then six-month renewal cycles where commercial terms can be re-opened at either party's request. Don't lock yourself into a five-year exclusive — they'll resent it, and resentment in tanish-bilish networks travels.

_Cross-product spread as a structural defense._ The strongest neutrality move is to sign across categories: one LDSP distributor (Imkon), one hardware distributor (Blum-side or Boyard), one glass distributor, one edge banding supplier. No single category has more than 25% of supplier revenue. This way, if Imkon walks, you lose 20–25% of supplier revenue but keep the platform alive and credible to workshops. Equally critical: visibly _talk_ about your hardware and glass partners in supplier meetings — it signals you're not anyone's captive.

_Multi-supplier optionality at the buyer level._ Your `r1_2` already says "buyers can technically add a second supplier." Make this default-on at signup, not buried in settings. Every workshop, by default, can order from any supplier listed on the platform. A workshop that's locked to one supplier is a workshop one supplier can hold hostage. The platform value is choice; defend it architecturally.

## (e) Currency, Tax, and Capital-Control Mechanics for Revenue Share

This is the part with the most legal landmines. Walk slowly.

**The hard rule on resident-to-resident payments in USD: don't.** Both Mebelchi (assuming Uzbek LLC, IT Park resident) and Imkon (Uzbek LLC) are residents. "The decree also bans payments in hard foreign currency for goods and services inside Uzbekistan." Settlements between residents must be in soum. Period. Paying Imkon a 2.5% revenue share in USD via your Uzbek bank account is a currency-control violation that would, if audited, trigger the standard penalty regime — and unlike the 180-day repatriation fines (5%/10%/35%), this one can also flag your IT Park residency for review. Don't risk the 0% tax status over a commission payment to Imkon.

**The hard rule on pegging UZS to USD: also don't, with a narrow workaround.** Pegging UZS prices to foreign currency or conventional units is prohibited under Article 9 of the Currency Regulation Law, except for some 2021 exceptions for strategic foreign investment projects. A clause that says "the commission is the UZS equivalent of $20 at the Central Bank rate on the date of invoice" is, strictly read, a violation. In practice, the tax authority enforces this loosely for small-ticket commercial contracts, but you don't want to be the test case. **Cleanest legal structure: denominate everything in soum. Recompute the soum price quarterly based on actual USD subscription revenue (which is itself converted to soum on receipt) and the Central Bank's average exchange rate over the quarter.** Document this calculation methodology in the contract. This way, you're not pegging — you're acknowledging a fluctuating soum number that happens to track FX.

**The practical structure for paying Imkon a "2.5% of buyer subscription revenue" commission:**

1. **Mebelchi LLC (IT Park resident) charges workshops in UZS** — even if the displayed price is "$9/mo" for marketing, the invoiced price is in soum at the published current rate. This is what BILLZ does too; their displayed pricing references USD-equivalent but invoicing is soum. "Foreign legal entities that supply e-services to individuals whereby the place of supply is considered Uzbekistan are subject to VAT in Uzbekistan." Since you're an Uzbek LLC selling to Uzbek workshops, you're domestic — VAT applies but you're exempt as an IT Park resident.
    
2. **Mebelchi's contract with Imkon is in soum.** The contract specifies a flat monthly listing fee in soum ("3,800,000 сум в месяц," roughly $300 at current rates) plus a per-workshop bounty in soum ("150,000 сум за каждую активированную мастерскую, плюс 35,000 сум в месяц на протяжении 12 месяцев"). These numbers are reviewable quarterly. No USD pegging.
    
3. **Mebelchi pays Imkon by bank transfer, soum to soum, against an Imkon-issued invoice.** Imkon's invoice will include VAT (12%) since they're not IT Park resident; you absorb that VAT in your gross commission cost. So budget commission as roughly 12% more than the headline number.
    
4. **For cross-border suppliers (Egger Hungary, Kronospan Austria, Blum Austria), the structure flips:** you contract directly with the foreign legal entity, denominate in EUR or USD, and pay them via your foreign-currency account. Repatriation rules and the 180-day window apply only to incoming export earnings, not to outgoing commission payments to non-residents. Withholding tax kicks in here — 15% on royalty-like payments to non-residents unless a double-taxation treaty applies. Uzbekistan has DTTs with ~50 countries including Austria, Germany, Russia, Turkey, China. The DTT rates on royalties are typically 5–10%. The form 1042-equivalent paperwork lives with your accountant; don't try to do it yourself.
    

**Where capital controls could change.** Uzbekistan has been liberalizing since 2017 and joined the IMF's Article VIII regime. The trajectory is towards more freedom, not less. The realistic risk over the next 24 months is not re-tightening of currency controls but stricter audit enforcement of the existing rules — particularly on price-pegging clauses, which the Tax Committee has signaled it wants to clean up. Hedge: keep all contracts in soum, document the methodology for FX-tracking adjustments transparently, and don't ever route commission payments offshore. WTO accession (target 2026–27) will push further liberalization, not the reverse.

**The unit economics consequence.** Your `r1_5` already computes $51 effective CAC per supplier-channel workshop on the $15 bounty + $3/month trailing model. After 12% VAT on supplier invoices (no IT Park benefit for the supplier), real CAC is closer to $57. Still cheap. The 2.5% revenue-share alternative — say a workshop pays $108/year, 2.5% = $2.70/year to Imkon — is mathematically smaller but, as I noted in section (c), it requires order-volume data Imkon won't share honestly. Stay with the bounty + trailing model.

(Translation: "Hello, [Name + patronymic]. My name is [name]. I was referred to you by [name of the person who introduced us]. The structure of that opener does five things: (1) drops the introducer's name early ("politics of naming"), (2) gives traction proof in one sentence, (3) explicitly disclaims selling, (4) frames you as someone with _information they don't have_ about their own small-customer segment, (5) ends with a permission ask, putting them in the position of granting. By the time you get to talking about commission, they've already been talking for 20 minutes about _their_ business, and the commission slot in the conversation feels natural.

**Pitch script for the value-prop moment** (when they ask "и что вы предлагаете?"):

> _"Мы предлагаем три вещи. Первое: мы размещаем Ваши SKU в нашем приложении, и каждый раз, когда мастер выбирает материал, Ваши товары с реальными ценами от [Имя поставщика] видны ему первыми по тем критериям, которые ему важны — цена, цвет, толщина. Вы знаете цены лучше нас, цены обновляете Вы сами через простую панель. Второе: на каждом счёте, который мастер выписывает клиенту через наше приложение, появляется маленькая отметка "материалы — [Имя поставщика]". Это бесплатная реклама в каждом контакте с конечным клиентом. Третье: за каждого мастера, который пришёл от Вас по QR-коду и оплатил три месяца, мы платим Вам 150,000 сум разово плюс 35,000 сум ежемесячно в течение года. На одной активной мастерской — 570,000 сум за год. Если у Вас 200 мастеров среди клиентов, и хотя бы 60 из них зайдут через Вас — это 34 миллиона сум в год без единого Вашего движения, кроме как разместить QR-код у кассы."_

(Translation: "We're offering three things. First: we list your SKUs in our app, and every time a master selects a material, your products with real prices from [Supplier name] are shown to him first based on the criteria that matter to him — price, color, thickness. You know the prices better than we do, and you update them yourself through a simple panel. Second: on every invoice that a master issues to his client through our app, a small mark appears: 'materials — [Supplier name]'. That's free advertising in every contact with the end client. The number 34 million soum (~$2,700/year incremental, free) is small enough to be believable, large enough to matter to a small distributor, and not so large it triggers "what's the catch" suspicion. Bigger distributors like Imkon will hear it and ask: "а если 600 мастеров?" (and if 600 masters?). Answer with the actual math, keep it grounded.

**Closing the first meeting.** Don't ask for a signature. Ask for permission to come back. _"Можно я приду через две недели с детальным предложением на бумаге? И я бы попросил Вас за это время подумать, какие у Вас есть вопросы — и я готов отвечать на самые жёсткие."_ ("May I come back in two weeks with a detailed written offer? And I would ask you during that time to think about what questions you have — I'm ready to answer the toughest ones.") Two weeks is the right cadence — long enough to signal you're not pushy, short enough that you stay in mind.

## Days 31–60: Move From Meeting to Pilot

**Second meetings (Days 31–45).** This is where the deal closes. Three things must happen:

_The objection round._ Every supplier has the same three objections. (i) "Why should we trust the data — what if you inflate the bounty numbers?" Answer: open dashboard, they see live workshop signups in real time with phone numbers redacted but verifiable count. Suggest random monthly audit by their internal accountant. (ii) "What if our competitor signs too?" Answer: yes, that's the plan — and you frame it as "если бы Вы попросили нас не работать с конкурентами, мы бы отказали — это та самая нейтральность, которая делает наш сервис ценным" (if you asked us not to work with competitors, we'd refuse — that's exactly the neutrality that makes our service valuable). (iii) "What if you fail and disappear?" Answer: the contract has a 60-day notice for them, no penalty; their downside is zero; here's our IT Park resident certificate; here's our Telegram presence in the master community.

_The negotiation._ They will ask for one of: better bounty, longer trailing, branded color theme, first-rank placement, or a "discount" on the listing fee. Concede on color theme (small visual customization in the supplier dashboard is cheap) and on listing fee for the first 6 months (waive it as a "пилотный период"). Hold the line on placement neutrality and bounty rates — these are your structural moats.

_The chai-to-handshake transition._ You will know the deal is closing when the supplier introduces you to their second-in-command, sales manager, or the accountant who will actually sign invoices. That's the signal to deliver the printed contract — bilingual, simple, one page if possible. Sign it on the spot if they're ready. If they want to "посмотреть с юристом" (look at it with a lawyer), schedule the follow-up signing within 7 days, not later.

**Pilot integration (Days 45–60).** For each signed supplier, three deliverables in seven days post-signing: (1) supplier login to a basic dashboard showing aggregated workshop counts and bounty accruals, (2) printed QR-code stickers and counter-card materials with the supplier's logo in their warehouse, (3) one in-person training session with their counter staff (15 minutes, scripted: "если мастер заказывает 1–3 листа, спросите 'Вы пользуетесь Mebelchi? Если ещё нет — сосканируйте этот QR, у Вас будет наш каталог цен на телефоне и счёт клиенту автоматически'"). Record this training; it becomes the template for supplier 2 and 3.

## Days 61–90: Close Two More, Build the Operating Cadence

**Suppliers 2 and 3 (Days 61–75).** With supplier 1 live and bounty payouts flowing, you now have a real reference. The opener for the next supplier changes:

> _"Здравствуйте, [Имя Отчество]. Я приходил месяц назад — мы тогда поговорили в общих чертах. С тех пор мы запустились с [Имя поставщика #1]. У них уже 12 мастеров оплатили через QR-код, бонус за это им начислен — 1.8 миллиона сум в первый месяц. Я подумал, что теперь у нас есть что показать на бумаге, и хотел бы вернуться к разговору."_

("Hello, [Name + patronymic]. I came by a month ago — we spoke in general terms then. Since then, we've launched with [Supplier #1]. They already have 12 masters who paid through their QR code, and bonus has been credited — 1.8 million soum in the first month. I thought we now have something to show on paper, and I'd like to return to the conversation.")

The "politics of naming" is now load-bearing in your pitch. Name a competitor they take seriously. The follow-up close rate on supplier 2 and 3, with supplier 1 already live as a credible reference, should be materially higher — call it 50–60% close rate on first follow-up vs 20–30% cold.

**Operating cadence by Day 90.** You should have:

- Three signed supplier contracts (one LDSP, one hardware, one glass or edge banding — categorical spread, not three LDSP)
- One supplier already 30+ days into pilot, with measurable workshop signups via their QR code (target: 15+ paid workshops attributed to supplier channel)
- A monthly invoice-and-payout cadence locked: the 5th of each month you generate supplier reports (aggregated, redacted to protect workshop privacy), they send you an invoice in soum, you pay within 7 days
- One Telegram channel created — "Поставщики Mebelchi" — with the three supplier reps in it, where you publish monthly aggregate numbers and answer questions. This channel becomes the social glue between you and the supplier ecosystem; tanish-bilish networks function partly through these visible, repeated, low-stakes interactions.
- A no-touch monthly soum-to-USD reconciliation methodology, drafted with your accountant, documented in your books, ready to defend at any audit.

The honest measure of success at Day 90 is not the contracts themselves — those are easy enough to get if your introducer chain is sound. It's whether supplier 1 spontaneously called supplier 2 to compare notes about working with you, and whether what they said made supplier 2 _more_ likely to sign. That's tanish-bilish working in your favor, and that's the only durable distribution moat in this market.

# r1_6 — Mebelchi Master Certification

**Question:** Can a certification academy be a primary acquisition channel for a $9–15/month mobile SaaS sold to skeptical, low-literacy, 40–55-year-old furniture shop owners in Uzbekistan?

**Short answer:** Yes — but the model that works for us is Lincoln Electric (since 1917, 150,000+ welders trained, dominant in arc welding), not HubSpot Academy. The HubSpot/Salesforce analog is mostly wrong for this audience and will fail if copied directly. The right design fuses three things: a peer-instructor apprenticeship model, a public skill-validated status ladder ("Master Mebelchi" tiers), and a tight loop into supplier partnerships and the Telegram cluster strategy already in r1_4 and r1_2.

This document is a synthesis of the public case-study evidence on education-led B2B SaaS, the economics of running a certification program at our price point, and a 6-month rollout plan to 100 certified mebelchi by month 6, 500 by month 12.

---

## 1. The case studies, ranked by relevance to us

Most "education as customer acquisition" writing online is about HubSpot and Salesforce. Both are wrong analogs for our audience — but each contributes a specific mechanism worth stealing. The closest analog is Lincoln Electric. The most directly transferable design is ServiceTitan's. Here is the ranked breakdown.

### 1.1 Lincoln Electric Welding School — the right analog (★★★★★)

**The play:** Since 1917, Lincoln Electric has run a vertically-integrated welding school in Cleveland. They have a 130,000 sq ft training facility, a regional network of partner schools (LEEPS — Lincoln Electric Education Partner Schools), and a tiered distributor education program ("Honors Program") with four levels combining online modules and in-person training. More than 150,000 men and women have been trained in arc welding technology, techniques, and safety practices since 1917. The school is operated as a state-regulated career college, not a marketing function.

**Why this is our analog, not HubSpot:**

- Blue-collar audience, hands-on skill, equipment-bound
- Status comes from validated craft skill, not from a digital badge on a profile
- The school predates the product becoming dominant — and is the reason the product became dominant
- Distributors are trained for free, then sell harder because they understand the gear
- The certification doubles as a customer pipeline (graduates buy Lincoln equipment because that's what they trained on) and a moat (anyone trying to displace Lincoln has to retrain the workforce)

**What we steal:** Equipment-anchored teaching ("learn on this CNC + this app together"), distributor-tier training (suppliers as trained partners), tiered curriculum (basic → advanced), and the patient century-long view — Lincoln did not need this to "pay back in 18 months." That said, we will need it to pay back faster.

### 1.2 ServiceTitan Certified User & Provider Program — the right structure (★★★★★)

**The play:** ServiceTitan is the dominant SaaS for home services contractors (HVAC, plumbing, electrical). Their audience is structurally similar to ours: 40–55-year-old shop owners with skilled-trades workers, mostly non-college, who run small-to-mid businesses and distrust software. ServiceTitan runs a multi-tier program:

- **ServiceTitan Certified User** — role-based, self-paced, free, with progressive badges (Foundational → Intermediate → Advanced → Certified User). The structure has three sub-badges before earning the master title.
- **ServiceTitan Certified Provider (STCP)** — for outside consultants who help contractors implement; rigorous training and testing.
- **App Marketplace certification** — third-party software vendors get certified to integrate.
- **Annual recertification** — every November/December to keep the badge valid.

**What's worth noting:** ServiceTitan does not publish acquisition share numbers, but the certified-consultant ecosystem (Powerhouse, BDR, Titan Pro, Go Time, etc.) is now a substantial channel — independent consultants prospect and sell ServiceTitan to contractors and get paid by both sides. This is the "trained outside evangelist" model.

**What we steal:** The three-level structure with sub-badges, the role-based design (the owner certification is different from the CNC operator certification is different from the assembler certification), annual recertification as a retention mechanism, and crucially the **Certified Provider** layer — which becomes our model for turning advanced mebelchi into paid local instructors.

### 1.3 HubSpot Academy — the retention mechanism, not the acquisition story (★★★☆☆)

**The play:** HubSpot Academy is the most famous customer-education program in software, with 200,000+ certified professionals and 165,000+ on the platform.

**The data that matters:**

- Certified users showed a **13% increase in customer revenue retention** vs non-certified — this is the direct, peer-reviewed number from HubSpot's Sr. Director of HubSpot Academy.
- HubSpot Academy serves as both a customer-success engine AND a top-of-funnel for non-customers (the certifications are open to everyone, which builds inbound interest in HubSpot).

**Why it's a partial analog:** The audience is mostly white-collar marketers who want a LinkedIn-credible badge for career advancement. Our mebelchi do not have a LinkedIn profile. They have a mahalla, a Telegram contact list, and a physical workshop wall.

**What we steal:** The retention mechanism. Once we have early customers, certifying them measurably improves retention — the 13% number is conservative and consistent with broader industry research (next section). For us, this matters more than the "acquisition through education" angle because at our churn-sensitive price point ($9/month, 3.5% churn target) every retention point is leveraged.

### 1.4 Salesforce Trailhead / Trailblazer Community — the community model (★★★☆☆)

**The play:** Trailhead is a gamified, free online learning platform with points, badges, and ranks. The Trailblazer Community runs 1,300+ local and virtual community groups in 90+ countries. 80% of Trailblazers report that engaging in the Community helps extend their Salesforce capabilities, increase efficiency, and reduce costs. An IDC report projected the Salesforce ecosystem would create 9.3 million new jobs and $1.6 trillion in business revenues by 2026.

**Why it's a partial analog:** Trailhead's structural genius is that getting certified literally makes you employable, which means thousands of people pursue certifications even before becoming a customer — and then they push their employers to buy Salesforce.

**What we steal:** Local community chapters (mahalla-based), gamified ranks (the Master Mebelchi ladder), and the insight that certifications become a status object the certified person actively promotes (LinkedIn for HubSpot/Salesforce, the workshop wall for us).

### 1.5 Procore Certification — the trade-targeted structure (★★★★☆)

**The play:** Procore (construction SaaS, mostly US) runs a free, self-paced certification program with role-based paths (Project Manager, Superintendent, Subcontractor, Owner, Architect, Admin). All courses are free, self-paced, online, with structured video + quizzes + exam. Certifications "do not expire" but recommend ongoing engagement.

**Why this is a strong analog:** Procore's customer is in construction — blue-collar adjacent, distrustful of software, similar age profile to ours. They give it away free and use it as the entry point to the platform.

**What we steal:** The free-forever model, the role-based design (owner vs operator vs assembler), and the lesson that for trades audiences, certifications are accepted as legitimate when they are anchored in real workflow tasks, not abstract concepts.

### 1.6 Gainsight Certified Admin — the conversion data (★★★☆☆)

**The data:** Gainsight published the cleanest published numbers tying certification to commercial outcomes. Among Gainsight customers:

- Companies with a Certified Admin: NPS 45 vs 31 (no certified admin)
- Executives at companies with a Certified Admin: NPS 69 vs 28
- **51% higher Expansion ARR per account** for clients with trained admins
- 3x higher "Healthy Active User" score

The mechanism: a trained admin is both an internal evangelist and a churn shield.

**What we steal:** Designate one certified person per workshop as the "shop admin" who is responsible for the implementation. Our equivalent of the "Certified Admin → 51% higher expansion" play is the "Certified Master → adds the second/third subscription seat as the shop grows."

### 1.7 The aggregated industry benchmarks

For sanity-checking our model:

- Customer education programs are increasing product retention by an average of 22%. Customer education programs are increasing product engagement by an average of 31%. The average customer education program increases customer satisfaction by 26%. 56% of companies that implement an education program see improved customer retention. The average company that invests in customer education sees a 16% decrease in support questions.
- 90% of companies have seen a positive return on their customer education investments. Companies with formalized education programs see 6.2% increase in revenue.
- Across 2025/2026 customer education and digital training programs, commonly reported benchmarks include ~6.2% revenue increase tied to stronger adoption/expansion motions and ~7.4% retention lift when education is embedded into onboarding plus ongoing enablement.
- According to Courtney Sembler, Senior Director of HubSpot Academy, certified users showed a 13% increase in customer revenue retention compared with non-certified users.

**Calibrated for our market:** assume our certification will move retention by 15–25% (higher than enterprise SaaS because our base churn is higher and the trust-deficit is the binding constraint). At our pricing, a 15% retention lift on a $9–15 ARPU base translates roughly to LTV $300 → $345–375 — every certified customer is worth ~$50–75 more.

## 2. The economics of a free certification program at our price point

The hard math, in our currency. The starting frame is what these programs _cost_, not what they earn — because at $9–15/month ARPU we have no margin for a Procore-sized program.

### 2.1 The unit-cost target

From r1_5: CAC target $30–50 per paying customer, LTV target $300+, LTV:CAC ratio 5:1+. Whatever the certification program costs us per certified student, the resulting acquisitions and retention lift must clear those bars.

### 2.2 Three cost models

|Model|Format|Cost per student (UZS-adjusted)|Cost per paying customer (60% activate → paid)|Verdict|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|**Online-only video course**|Self-paced video + Telegram-bot quiz|$3–8 (production amortized)|$5–14|Cheap but won't convert this audience|
|**Hybrid (video + 1-day in-person)**|4 video modules + 1 day on-site at partner workshop|$35–60 (instructor, venue, food, materials)|$58–100|Works; matches budget|
|**Full in-person bootcamp**|3-day on-site, hands-on, sticker workflow run live|$120–200|$200+|Too expensive at $9 ARPU|

**Recommendation:** Hybrid model. The in-person day is the conversion engine; the videos are the filter and the post-event reinforcement.

### 2.3 The conversion model

Adapting r1_5's funnel for the certification cohort:

```
Enrollment (free, Telegram signup)        100 mebelchi
→ Complete video modules                   75 (75%, gated quizzes)
→ Show up to 1-day in-person workshop      55 (73% — they've already invested 4hrs)
→ Pass practical exam (do a real cut list) 50 (91% — exam designed to be passable)
→ Activated trial (use product in shop)    45 (90% — the exam IS the activation)
→ Paid within 30 days                      33 (73% — vs 30–35% blended in r1_5)
→ Still paid at month 6                    27 (82% — vs 70% non-certified retention)
```

The certification cohort converts at roughly **2x the rate of cold acquisition** and retains at roughly **17 percentage points higher**. This is consistent with the 13–22% retention lift cited above, accounting for the higher-touch start.

**The CAC math:** Hybrid model costs ~$2,500–4,500 to run a single 100-student cohort (one in-person day, one instructor, simple venue). That produces ~33 paying customers in the first month. **CAC: $76–136 per paid customer** — above r1_5's $30–50 ceiling at first glance, but with two corrections:

1. **The certification cohort retention lift is real**: 27 paid at month 6 vs 22 for cold cohort = 5 saved customers × LTV $300 ≈ $1,500 in retained value
2. **The graduate flywheel**: certified mebelchi refer the next cohort at roughly 40–50% (matching Lincoln Electric and Salesforce community data). One certified shop owner brings on average 1.2 other mebelchi as enrollees over 12 months. By cohort 4 (month 8+), the program is largely self-feeding and effective CAC drops to $20–40.

This is the actual value proposition of the certification model: high upfront CAC that decays sharply as the network compounds. Pure field-visits CAC stays roughly constant. This is identical structurally to the Lincoln Electric advantage — the cost is amortized over a century, but the moat compounds.

### 2.4 What free vs paid certification looks like for us

The 2025 customer-education benchmarks data: ~29% of formal certification programs monetize, with another 38% planning to. At our market depth this is a sequencing question. **Year 1: free.** We need adoption velocity more than we need course revenue. **Year 2+: paid premium tier.** "Master Mebelchi" (year-3 graduate) pays $30 for advanced kromka + assembly module, gets income from teaching cohorts as an instructor. This is the Lincoln Electric "Honors Elite" parallel.

### 3.1 The curriculum (V1, deliverable by month 4)

**Course name:** Mebelchi Master Certification — Level 1 (the "Apprentice Master" / "Yosh Usta") **Length:** 4 video modules (15 min each) + 1 full-day in-person workshop + 1 practical exam.

By month 9 the first cohort of 25 graduates includes 2–3 candidates for "Certified Trainer" who get a daily fee ($50–100/day) to teach cohort 4+ in their region.

### 3.2 The credentialing structure (Master Mebelchi ladder)

Following the ServiceTitan three-level pattern, with Uzbek-language status terms:

- **Level 1 — Yosh Usta** (Apprentice Master): Passes the Level 1 exam. Receives a printed certificate signed by the factory friend + Oppoq, a wall-mountable badge for the workshop, and an in-app status icon on their profile. Eligible for $9 founding-customer pricing for life.
- **Level 2 — Usta** (Master): 6 months of active use, completed Wardrobe module, passed advanced exam. Eligible to refer other mebelchi (gets 1 month free per successful referral; capped at 12 free months/year). Listed in the public "Find a Master" directory.
- **Level 3 — Bosh Usta** (Head Master): 12+ months active use, certified trainer, has taught at least one workshop. Receives a paid teaching fee, a co-branded profile in the supplier marketplace, and 30% off all supplier listings on the platform.

The three-level structure compresses Salesforce's gamification and ServiceTitan's badge progression into a culturally-resonant Uzbek apprenticeship terminology. The ranking is real, not arbitrary — each level requires demonstrated work.

### 3.3 The referral mechanics (the evangelist loop)

This is where the design must be tight, because referrals are the only mechanism by which the certification program ever becomes self-funding.

**The referral structure:**

- A Level 2 Usta who refers another mebelchi who enrolls and pays for 1 month gets 1 month free (cap: 12 months/year)
- Every successful referral also adds a "Tarbiyalovchi" (Mentor) sub-badge — visible to the entire Telegram community
- After 5 successful referrals, the Usta is invited to the Bosh Usta track (paid teaching, supplier discount)
- Every certificate carries the referring Usta's name on the back ("Tarbiyachi: [name]") — this is the mahalla-network mechanism made literal

This converts the financial incentive into a status incentive, which works better with this audience. The cap at 12 months/year prevents abuse and ensures the economics still work.

### 3.4 The social proof loops

Three layers, each from a proven source:

1. **The wall certificate** (Lincoln Electric model): a beautifully designed paper certificate the mebelchi hangs in their workshop. Customers visiting the workshop see it. Other mebelchi visiting see it. Brand presence as moat — straight from `01_STRATEGY.md`.
2. **The Telegram badge** (Salesforce Trailhead model): a profile decoration in the `mebelchi_general` group and in the Mebelchi app's "Masters" tab. Public, dynamic, ranked. Sortable by Bosh Usta → Usta → Yosh Usta.
3. **The "Find a Master" directory** (ServiceTitan Certified Provider model): in the consumer-facing layer (or the supplier side), certified masters appear above non-certified shops when end-customers search "kitchen near me." This becomes a real revenue lever for them — and a real moat for us.

## 4. Why this works for our specific audience — the Uzbekistan adaptations

The HubSpot/Salesforce models assume the certification status object is visible _on LinkedIn_. Our adaptation is to swap the visibility layer to:

- **The workshop wall** (every customer + every visiting mebelchi sees the certificate)
- **The mahalla Telegram group** (every member sees the ranks and refers business)
- **The supplier counter** (Eman / Korzinka mebel zone suppliers see who is certified and route bulk-order leads accordingly)

### 4.1 Bilingual delivery

Every video is filmed twice — once in Uzbek by the factory friend, once in Russian by Oppoq or a Russian-native instructor. Both stay live. Many mebelchi switch between languages mid-workshop. The exam is bilingual; the certificate is bilingual on a single page (Uzbek primary, Russian secondary). This is non-negotiable and tracks `03_PRODUCT_V1.md` principle #4.

### 4.2 The mahalla-network distribution model

The mahalla (neighborhood/community council) is a real organizing unit in Uzbekistan — particularly in the dense industrial mebel areas (Sergeli, Yangiobod, Korzinka mebel zone in Tashkent; similar zones in Samarkand and Andijon).

The distribution play:

- **Month 1–2:** Identify the 5–8 mahalla zones with the highest mebel-shop density. Use the 30-shop field-immersion notes from `08_EXECUTION_30D.md` to pick.
- **Month 3:** Host the first in-person certification day in the densest zone (Sergeli or equivalent). Use a partner supplier's warehouse as the venue (free, supplier wins reputation). The factory friend personally invites 30 shops in the zone via WhatsApp/Telegram. 20 show up.
- **Month 4–6:** Each subsequent cohort happens in a different mahalla zone, hosted at a different supplier. The geographic spread is deliberate — the certificate displayed on workshop wall #1 is seen by every visiting customer and supplier rep, creating natural pull in the surrounding 2–3 shop blocks.

This is the "20 shop owners, all within a 10-minute drive, all see each other's workshop walls" mechanism. It is exactly what Lincoln Electric did with regional partner schools, compressed to neighborhood-scale.

### 4.3 The supplier integration

This connects directly to `-r1_2_partnership.md` and `-r1_5_revenue.md`. Each certified mebelchi cohort happens _at_ a supplier's warehouse, with the supplier's branding co-displayed. The supplier:

- Provides the venue (free)
- Catches lunch ($5–10/head)
- Gets first-look access to the cohort (these 20 newly trained mebelchi are now buying from them)
- Gets co-branded mentions on every certificate ("Powered by [Supplier] + Mebelchi")
- Receives the bounty structure from r1_5 ($10–20 per activated-paid + $2–3/mo for 12 months)

This means **the supplier funds the program directly via their warehouse + lunch + the bounty payments**. Our incremental cost per cohort drops to: instructor day-rate ($100–200) + printed certificates ($2 each × 20 = $40) + practice CNC time (free, supplier or factory-friend provides). Roughly **$200–400 per 20-student cohort, of which roughly half is the instructor**. That's $10–20 per certified student — meaningfully below the $35–60 in section 2.2 because the supplier subsidizes the rest.

### 4.4 Why the in-person component is non-negotiable here

Pure online certification (HubSpot Academy model) fails in this audience for three reasons:

1. **The user is not comfortable with self-directed video study.** Field immersion notes will confirm: many 50-year-old mebelchi watched maybe ten YouTube tutorials total, and finished none. Their learning model is apprenticeship — show me, then I do it next to you.
2. **The trust transfer requires presence.** A factory friend handing them a certificate in person, with photos taken, is worth 100× the same digital credential. This is from `01_STRATEGY.md`: "Founder isn't a mebelchi" — the in-person day is how we cross that gap.
3. **The hardware demo is the conversion event.** The first time the mebelchi sees the sticker get printed and read by a real CNC, that is the moment they convert. We cannot deliver that over Zoom.

## 6. What can kill this program

In order of likelihood, and how we mitigate:

1. **The first cohort doesn't show up.** Mitigation: factory friend personally calls each enrollee 48 hours before. Free lunch and a printed certificate is a hard offer to refuse for a 50-year-old craftsman.
2. **Graduates don't pay.** Mitigation: the exam IS the activation event. They've already used the product to pass the test. The conversion from "I just passed" to "I'll pay $9/month for this" is short because they've felt the value in their hands.
3. **The certificate becomes worthless because we hand them out too easily.** Mitigation: 25–30% exam failure rate is healthy. Failure means "retake next cohort" — they come back, they pay another month of attention, they tell their mahalla "the exam is real."
4. **A cohort instructor leaves and takes the brand with them.** Mitigation: brand-design moat (`01_STRATEGY.md`) — the certificate, the badge, the directory all live in the Mebelchi brand, not the instructor's name. Lincoln Electric is the institution, not any individual instructor.
5. **Suppliers see the certification as a cost-suck.** Mitigation: every cohort produces an analytics report for the supplier showing how many of "their" mebelchi became active users. This is the r1_5 dashboard pattern, applied to the certification channel.

# -r1_7 — CNC Machine Dealer Partnerships as Distribution Channel

**Version:** 1.0 **Status:** Research synthesis. Sits alongside `-r1_2_partnership.md` (material suppliers), `-r1_5_revenue.md` (funnel math), `-r1_6_community_moat.md` (Telegram distribution). This document covers the **third distinct distribution channel** — industrial machine resellers.

---

## The thesis in one sentence

A CNC machine dealer's interest is selling more $20K–$40K machines, not earning $3/month rev-share on a $15 SaaS. To make the dealer channel work at your price point, the software has to function as **a sales-closing tool for the dealer**, not as a product the dealer sells. Bundle it free with every machine sold, pay the dealer a one-time $100–$300 spiff per activated install, and capture the rev-share only after month 12 — when the dealer has already moved on to the next machine sale and the customer is on the hook for the subscription.

Romchi did roughly this dance on the window side with AKFA-adjacent machinery dealers. The mebelchi side has more dealers, less consolidation, and Chinese OEM economics that _favor_ you — Chinese dealers fight hard for share and will bundle software aggressively to differentiate against their direct competitors.

---

## Why this channel matters at all (and why it differs from supplier partnerships)

Your existing supplier-partnership work in `-r1_2_partnership.md` and `-r1_5_revenue.md` covers material warehouses — recurring buyer relationships, weekly transactions, share-of-wallet leverage. Machine dealers are structurally different:

|Dimension|Material supplier (LDSP/MDF/hardware)|CNC machine dealer (Excitech/KDT/Nanxing/Homag)|
|---|---|---|
|Transaction frequency with shop|Weekly|Once every 5–10 years|
|Per-transaction value|$200–$5,000|$15,000–$80,000 (Chinese) / $50K–$200K (Homag)|
|Relationship duration after sale|Indefinite (re-orders)|12-month warranty + service contracts|
|What they sell|Materials + price-list discipline|Capex + financing + service + spare parts|
|Their #1 fear|Buyer defects to competitor warehouse|Buyer defects to competitor brand on next machine|
|What you offer them|Sticky workflow keeps buyers in their channel|A reason for the buyer to choose _their_ machine over a rival|

**This means the supplier playbook and the dealer playbook are not the same play with the volume turned up.** Suppliers want operational lock-in. Dealers want differentiation at point of sale. You design the partnership around what they actually want.

The dealer channel sits _above_ the supplier channel in funnel order: a shop typically buys a CNC once, then buys materials weekly for 10 years. Capturing the shop at the moment of CNC purchase is the most valuable touchpoint in the entire industry. From `-r1_5_revenue.md`: supplier referrals generate 700 of your target 1,000 paid shops. The dealer channel generates _fewer_ shops but the _highest-LTV_ shops — the ones who just spent $25K and are about to scale.

---

## (a) The exact commercial structures used

Across the woodworking CAD/CAM ecosystem, five commercial structures recur. Each has a distinct economic logic and a distinct failure mode.

### Structure 1: Captive Distributor (Stiles–Homag–Microvellum/iX model)

The most aggressive form. Homag owns Stiles Machinery 100% (acquired in 2014). Stiles is the _exclusive_ North American provider of Homag machinery and software. When you buy a Homag CNC router from Stiles, the Homag iX CAD/CAM software is sold as part of the package by the same sales engineer.

- **Economics for the dealer (Stiles):** Standard machinery margin (industry estimate: 15–25% on a $80K–$200K machine = $12K–$50K), plus the iX software license markup, plus annual maintenance contracts on both.
- **Economics for the software (iX):** Captive distribution to Homag customers in NA, with imos (the software partner Homag acquired) handling the CAD engine. Software is functionally a feature of the machine purchase, not a separate product evaluation.
- **Why it works:** Single P&L. Sales rep is compensated for both machine and software. Customer sees one quote, one demo, one support number.
- **Why it doesn't apply to you:** You can't acquire a Chinese CNC dealer in Tashkent. And the captive model only works when the parent owns the distribution arm.
- **What you steal:** The single-quote single-demo positioning. When a dealer demos a $25K Excitech to a mebelchi, the demo should include "and the software is already loaded, free for the first year."

### Structure 2: Ecosystem Partner (Microvellum–Anderson/Biesse/Holz-Her/Multicam/SCM/Weeke model)

The most replicable. Microvellum publicly lists machine OEM partners — Anderson, Biesse, C.R. Onsrud, Holz-Her, Homag, Multicam, SCM, Schelling, Weeke, plus the Chinese players Excitech and KDT. They maintain an **open-source post-processor library** that lets any of those machines accept Microvellum output without per-machine licensing fees.

- **Economics for the dealer:** Not direct compensation. Indirect benefit: "Microvellum supports your machine" is a checkbox in the sales conversation. Dealer wins more sales because the software ecosystem reduces buyer anxiety. Microvellum customers buying a machine often ask their Microvellum rep "which machine do you recommend?" — informal referral flow.
- **Economics for Microvellum:** No royalty paid to the dealer. Microvellum is monetized on the customer side ($5K–$20K perpetual + maintenance). The dealer ecosystem is a _moat_ — competitors can't easily replicate dozens of validated post-processors.
- **Why it works:** Decoupled relationships. Microvellum sells direct, dealer sells direct, both products are stronger because the other exists.
- **What you steal:** The post-processor + machine compatibility matrix as a public asset. Your equivalent: a public page listing every CNC brand your DXF/MPR output is validated against, with the dealer's logo and contact info. Free SEO for the dealer, free credibility for you. Mozaik runs the same play with 175+ free stock post-processors.

### Structure 3: Pure Reseller Margin (Vectric–ToolsToday–Sienci–Aspire-Vectric model)

Vectric (Aspire $1,995 one-time, V-Carve Pro $699) operates through a network of authorized resellers: ToolsToday, MakerTechStore, Sienci Labs, plus dozens of regional CNC machine retailers. Resellers earn a discount margin — public retail is $1,995, reseller cost is significantly lower, and resellers compete on price (some sell at $119 — likely volume reseller deals or grey-market keys).

- **Economics for the dealer/reseller:** 10–25% margin on each license sold, paid as a discount off retail rather than a commission. Reseller invoices the end customer.
- **Economics for Vectric:** Lower per-unit revenue, but the reseller network is global, multilingual, and embedded in CNC machine purchasing journeys.
- **Why it works for one-time licenses:** Reseller "owns" the customer relationship for support. Customer doesn't care about the upstream relationship.
- **Why it doesn't fit your model:** Subscription SaaS at $9–15/month means a 20% margin is $1.80–$3/month — not enough to motivate a reseller. The reseller model assumes a one-time license with a meaningful upfront payment.

### Structure 4: SPIFF + Trailing Commission (the structure you should use)

Adapted from SPIFF (Sales Performance Incentive Fund) programs documented in B2B channel literature. The dealer's frontline sales rep earns a short-term cash bonus per qualified sale, while the dealer organization earns a longer-term recurring share.

Standard structure across documented programs:

- **Per-machine SPIFF:** $50–$500 to the _individual sales rep_ (not the dealer entity) when the software is installed and activated on a sold machine. Paid 30–60 days after install to filter out fraud and ghost installs.
- **Trailing commission to the dealer entity:** 15–25% of recurring SaaS revenue for 12–24 months on customers attributed to that dealer.
- **MDF (Marketing Development Fund):** 3–5% of partner-sourced revenue available as a co-marketing budget, requiring formal plans and performance metrics (per documented industry benchmarks).

For your numbers at $15/month:

- SPIFF to sales rep: $100–$200 per machine sold (one-time, paid month 2)
- Trailing commission to dealer entity: 20% of $15/month = $3/month for 12 months = $36 per customer
- Total CAC paid to channel per converted customer: $136–$236
- LTV at 5% monthly churn and $15 ARPA: $300
- LTV:CAC: 1.3:1 to 2.2:1 — tight but workable, and improves dramatically if you can hold churn under 3%

**Why this beats pure rev-share:** The dealer's frontline rep gets a real human dopamine hit at month 2 (the $100–$200 spiff), which is what changes selling behavior. The dealer's CFO gets a recurring revenue line that justifies allocating training time. The 12-month cliff means you stop paying the dealer once the customer has decided to stick around independently — which protects your unit economics at scale.

### Structure 5: White-label / Co-branded (high-risk, high-reward)

The dealer rebrands your software as their own ("Excitech Studio" running on your backend). This is how some Chinese CNC OEMs have historically operated — bundled-software is presented as a feature of the machine, not a third-party product.

- **Economics:** Dealer captures 50–70% of revenue (sometimes 100% on the first 12 months, then split). You become invisible infrastructure.
- **Why it works:** Massive distribution amplification. Excitech sells 500 machines/year in Russia + CIS; even at 30% bundling rate, that's 150 new shops/year per OEM relationship.
- **Why it's dangerous:** You lose direct customer relationship, brand, churn data, and pricing power. If the OEM decides to build their own version (and Chinese OEMs frequently do), you're cut off overnight with no remediation.
- **What you do with it:** Offer it as a _paid upgrade tier_ to the dealer relationship — Tier 1 partner gets co-branded "Mebelchi by [Dealer]" treatment after they hit a threshold (e.g., 50 activated installs in a year), in exchange for a 3-year exclusivity-light agreement.

### Summary table — what to offer your first Chinese CNC distributor

|Component|Recommended structure|Dollar value|
|---|---|---|
|Bundled license|12 months free with every machine sold|Your COGS: ~$10/customer/year cloud cost|
|Per-machine SPIFF to rep|One-time, paid month 2 after activation|$100–$200|
|Trailing commission to dealer|20% of MRR, months 13–24 only|~$36/year per retained customer|
|MDF co-marketing budget|3% of partner-sourced revenue, refundable on Tashkent demo events|$50–$200/quarter to start|
|Post-processor compatibility|Free Excitech / KDT / Nanxing certified output|Engineering cost only|
|Co-branded skin (Tier 1)|Unlocked after 50 activations/year|Free to dealer, 3-year retention clause|

This is honest economics, not a pitch deck. The dealer's first question will be: "what's my real cash compensation per machine?" The answer is $100–$236 plus a free differentiation in their sales pitch.

## (b) Case studies — companies that achieved meaningful revenue through machine dealer channels

Concrete examples are rare because most CAD/CAM vendors don't publicly disclose channel attribution. What follows is reconstructed from public sources, partner pages, and industry reporting.

### Case 1: Microvellum — distributed ecosystem, $50M+ ARR

Microvellum is the most successful pure-play example of the ecosystem-partner model. They run on AutoCAD (Autodesk's platform), and have built validated integrations with virtually every commercial CNC brand in woodworking, including Excitech and KDT for the Chinese segment.

**What's public:**

- Microvellum has been profitable and growing since at least 2010, with no outside funding disclosed.
- The Woodshop News profile listed Microvellum's connection to Anderson, Biesse, C.R. Onsrud, Holz-Her, Homag, Multicam, SCM, Schelling, Weeke — basically every major woodworking CNC brand in North America.
- They run an "open source post-processor solution" — anyone (including dealers) can write or modify a post-processor without paying Microvellum.

**Inferred channel economics:**

- Pricing public estimates: $5K–$15K perpetual license + 15–20% annual maintenance. At a midpoint of $10K + $2K/year, every customer they win is worth ~$25K over 5 years.
- They don't pay dealer commissions. They pay dealer _attention_ — they show up at dealer events, support dealer training, and make sure their software runs flawlessly on every dealer's machines.
- Dealer-influenced revenue percentage is not disclosed, but anecdotal industry estimates put it at 30–50% of new logo acquisition.

**Transferable lesson:** You don't need to pay every dealer. You need to be the software the dealer's sales engineer mentions in slide 12 of their machine deck without being prompted. That happens when you've done the technical work (post-processors), shown up at dealer events, and trained the dealer's support team for free.

### Case 2: Stiles / Homag iX — captive bundle, ~70% North American Homag attach rate

Homag iX is Homag's proprietary CAD/CAM platform, developed via the imos partnership and sold in North America exclusively through Stiles Machinery (Homag's wholly-owned distributor).

**What's public:**

- Stiles introduced HOMAG iX to the US market and Stiles markets it as core to the Homag value proposition.
- iX claims to serve "over 4,000 designers and thousands more end-use customers worldwide."
- Public messaging: "save up to 70% of the time from customer concept to production process" — the software is framed as the productivity unlock, not a separate product.

**Inferred channel economics:**

- The Homag machine costs $80K–$300K. The iX software adds $5K–$15K to the quote, plus annual maintenance.
- Attach rate (% of Homag buyers who also buy iX) is industry-estimated at 60–80% in North America because the sales engineer demos them together.
- Channel conflict is structurally impossible because there is no separate channel — Stiles is Homag, and Homag is Stiles.

**Transferable lesson:** The closest analog to "captive distribution" you can manufacture without owning a dealer is _exclusivity within a window_ — sign one anchor Chinese CNC distributor in Tashkent for a 12-month exclusive-bundle agreement, then expand to others after the model is validated.

### Case 3: Cabinet Vision (Hexagon) — multi-channel reseller with dealer integration

Cabinet Vision is owned by Hexagon AB (the Swedish industrial group that also owns multiple CAD/CAM brands; their parent acquired Vero Software in 2014, which then included Cabinet Vision).

**What's public:**

- Cabinet Vision is distributed in North America via Stiles Machinery (yes, the same Stiles) and also via independent dealer partners.
- Hexagon's Manufacturing Intelligence division generated >$1B revenue in 2023; Cabinet Vision is a smaller line within that division but profitable.
- Public partner page on Hexagon lists "Don Bigelow — Partner Relations" specifically for Cabinet Vision, suggesting a dedicated partner-management function.

**Inferred channel economics:**

- Cabinet Vision license: $5K–$10K + 15–20% maintenance.
- Dealer margin estimates: 20–30% standard reseller cut on initial license, 10–15% on maintenance renewals.
- Cyncly acquired Mozaik in 2022 (per Woodworking Network), consolidating Mozaik into a larger holding that also owns 2020 Design, Mastercam-adjacent properties, and others — suggesting that scale economics in this channel structure are real.

**Transferable lesson:** Even with a multi-channel distribution model, you need a named human in your org whose job is dealer relations. By the time you have 3 dealer partners, that role is full-time.

### Case 4: Mozaik (Cyncly) — post-processor-as-distribution

Mozaik takes an unusual position: they offer 175+ free stock post-processors covering virtually every CNC machine brand, including Chinese OEMs. First hour of custom post integration is free; additional hours are $95/hour.

**What's public:**

- Mozaik Manufacturing $125/mo, Mozaik CNC $145/mo, Mozaik Enterprise $325/mo (current 2024–2026 pricing).
- 3-month paid trial period (not free trial).
- Acquired by Cyncly in 2022.
- Customer reviews on SourceForge mention pricing increases of 75%+ post-Cyncly acquisition — a cautionary tale about what happens when a SaaS gets PE-owned.

**Inferred channel economics:**

- Mozaik's distribution is more direct than Microvellum's, but they openly invite dealers to participate via "your machinery supplier can modify the post as necessary."
- The free post-processor library functions as a dealer-friendliness signal: a dealer in Tashkent doesn't need to pay Mozaik to make Mozaik work with their Excitech.
- $95/hour post-processor integration is a small but real revenue line that mostly flows to Mozaik's own engineers, occasionally subcontracted.

**Transferable lesson:** Free post-processors are cheap to maintain and create enormous goodwill with dealers. Build Excitech, KDT, Nanxing, Homag (yes, even Homag — at least the universal-DXF entry point) as first-class supported outputs. Per `02_MARKET_AND_MACHINES.md`, you've already locked DXF as your universal output, so this is operationally cheap.

### Case 5: Vectric (one-time license, global reseller network)

Vectric is the exception that proves the rule. They run a perpetual-license model (Aspire $1,995 one-time, V-Carve $699), distributed through dozens of resellers globally, including hobby CNC sellers like Sienci, Onefinity-adjacent retailers, and industrial CNC distributors.

**What's public:**

- Aspire retails for $1,995 (latest price published). Resellers like ToolsToday bundle it with Amana tooling at a 10% discount.
- Strong user community on woodworking forums — community is part of the moat, similar to dbt.
- 30-day refund + 90-day replacement guarantee through resellers.
- Reseller pricing visible in some channels at heavy discounts ($119 from grey-market resellers; legitimate authorized resellers like Sienci sell at full retail).

**Inferred channel economics:**

- Reseller margin: industry-standard 10–25% for software licenses, paid as up-front discount.
- Vectric makes a chunk of their revenue from upgrade fees and from new licenses going to first-time buyers, not from large enterprise contracts.

**Transferable lesson:** A one-time-license model is fundamentally easier to sell through dealers than a subscription. _You won't get this advantage at $15/month._ What you can do: offer the dealer a one-time-paid "lifetime founder license" option to bundle ($199 or $299 one-shot), specifically for the founding cohort, with the dealer earning 30% of that $199–$299 immediately. This converts the SPIFF into commission.

### Case 6: Biesse Dealer Academy + B_SOLID — vendor-led training as moat

Biesse runs a formal Dealer Academy out of Pesaro, Italy. In 2018 they hosted distributors from globally for training on CNC machines, edge banders, sanders, and panel saws, including training on the SOPHIA IoT app and B_SOLID CAD/CAM. WOODWEB users have noted B_SOLID is bundled at training time with Biesse machines, and dealer reps have offered freelance B_SOLID training services in the aftermarket.

**Inferred channel economics:**

- B_SOLID is bundled with Biesse machines at no separate license cost in most regions (varies). Annual maintenance is a smaller line item than the machine itself.
- Dealer "training services" become a profit center for both the dealer (paid training contracts to end customers, $1K–$5K per training engagement) and Biesse (machine sales tied to validated software).

**Transferable lesson:** Training is a profit center for the dealer. If your software is _easy enough that a dealer rep can train a customer in 90 minutes_, then the dealer makes margin on the training service — even when the software itself is free for the customer's first year. This is the strongest non-cash incentive you have. _Mebelchi is designed for this: big buttons, no training, 10-minute first kitchen._ That product principle from `03_PRODUCT_V1.md` is also a dealer-channel feature.

## (c) Training a CNC dealer's sales team to also pitch SaaS

This is where most software companies in this category fail. The dealer's salespeople are mechanical-engineering-trained reps who have spent 15 years selling capex. Asking them to sell SaaS using SaaS vocabulary doesn't work. The training has to be designed around their existing mental models.

### What fails (documented patterns)

1. **Treating the dealer rep like a SaaS AE.** Don't send them MQL/SQL definitions, demo decks with feature-comparison matrices, or NPS dashboards. They tune out within 5 minutes.
2. **Multi-day training programs.** Dealer reps' time is allocated to closing the next machine. A 3-day training in another country is a non-starter unless tied to an existing OEM event (e.g., the Biesse Pesaro academy is _only_ feasible because Biesse owns Pesaro).
3. **Software-pricing-only conversations.** A rep who closes a $25K machine has no interest in a 30-minute conversation about $15/month software — that's a rounding error on his deal.
4. **One-size-fits-all certification.** Per the channel literature, partner programs with rigid certification requirements ("Authorized requires one certified sales expert + one customer support expert") work for $5K+ ACV products. They don't work for $9–15/month products where the certification cost exceeds the lifetime commission.

### What works (synthesized from the case data)

#### Mechanism 1: The 5-Minute Sales Pitch Card

A laminated A5 card given to every dealer sales rep. Russian on one side, Uzbek on the other. Contains:

- The two-sentence sales positioning ("This is what makes our $25K Excitech worth $35K of any competitor's. The buyer gets free software for the first year that turns their phone into a control center.")
- The 3 customer questions the rep should always ask (existing CNC? phone or PC? kitchens or wardrobes?)
- A QR code that opens a 90-second video demo on the buyer's phone
- The 1-sentence ROI math ("Saves 8 sheets of MDF per month at $40/sheet = $320/month value, paid software is $15/month")

This card costs $0.30 to print. The dealer's sales rep keeps it in his shirt pocket forever.

#### Mechanism 2: Co-Demo at the Showroom

The dealer's showroom has the machine on the floor. You provide a tablet pre-loaded with the app, demo-ready cut lists, and one printed sample sticker. When the rep does a machine demo, the _last 5 minutes_ are the software demo. Not "and we also have software" — but "and the machine you just saw runs from a phone using software that's free for 12 months when you buy today."

This compresses the software pitch to inside the machine pitch, which is the only context the dealer rep can productively sell from.

#### Mechanism 3: The Activation Bounty (not a quota)

Don't give the dealer a sales quota for the software. Give them an activation bounty for every machine sold:

- Machine sold + software installed within 7 days = $100 to the rep, paid in cash by month 2
- Machine sold + software installed + customer paid month 13 = additional $50 to the rep, paid in month 14

This rewards the actual outcome you care about (customer retention through year 1), without burdening the rep with selling effort he doesn't have time for.

#### Mechanism 4: Train the Service Engineer, Not the Sales Rep

The dealer's service engineer goes to the customer's shop after the machine is installed. He's there for 2–4 days commissioning the machine. _That's the optimal training moment_ — for the customer and for the engineer himself. Train the service engineer to spend the last 90 minutes of his commissioning trip setting up the software for the customer. Pay him personally $50 per setup completed. The engineer becomes your fieldnut field salesperson without being a salesperson.

This was hinted at in Biesseworks/B_SOLID training threads on WOODWEB — service engineers often become the customer's primary contact for software questions in practice, even when sales reps "own" the relationship on paper.

#### Mechanism 5: Quarterly Lunch + Update

Once per quarter, you (or your factory friend per `01_STRATEGY.md`) take the dealer's full sales team to lunch. 1 hour. You demo new features for 15 minutes, listen to their complaints for 30 minutes, hand out the new pocket cards for the next quarter. This is a $200 lunch that converts to a structurally better quarter of channel performance.

### What to actually deliver to the dealer in week 1

The minimum starter kit:

1. 50 printed A5 pocket cards (Russian/Uzbek)
2. One demo tablet with app + sample data + Wi-Fi hotspot
3. A 90-second product video, in Russian and Uzbek, hosted on a QR-coded landing page
4. A spreadsheet template the rep can fill in to compute "machine + software ROI" for any specific customer (3 input fields, 1 output number)
5. One personalized URL per rep, so spiff attribution is unambiguous
6. A WhatsApp/Telegram group with you in it, where reps can ask questions and get answers within 4 hours

If you don't have those six things before pitching a dealer, don't pitch them yet.

## (d) Becoming the "default" software without violating exclusivity

The legal/regulatory constraint matters but is often overstated. Tying arrangements (where buying product A _requires_ buying product B) are illegal in most jurisdictions, but **bundling** (where buying A _includes_ B at no extra cost) is legal essentially everywhere. The difference is whether the customer has the option to decline B and pay less for A.

The key question in your target geographies:

- **Uzbekistan:** No active antitrust enforcement against software bundling in industrial equipment sales. The relevant law is the Law on Competition (1996, amended several times), but enforcement is concentrated in dominant-player abuse cases, not vendor-bundling. Verify with local counsel before signing — but the structural risk is low.
- **Kazakhstan:** Stronger competition law (modeled on EU framework), but bundling of complementary goods is permitted as long as the customer can in principle source the second good separately.
- **Kyrgyzstan:** Limited enforcement infrastructure. Practical risk near zero.
- **Turkey:** Most rigorous of your four target markets. The Competition Authority (Rekabet Kurumu) has prosecuted tying cases in pharma and energy. For Turkey specifically, structure the bundle as "free for 12 months with optional renewal" rather than "required for machine operation."

The structural fix that works in all four markets: **the software is bundled but separable**. The customer can decline the software bundle and the machine price doesn't change. In practice 95%+ accept the bundle because it's free. But the _legal option_ to decline is what immunizes the structure.

### How you actually become default

Three mechanisms, each operating on a different timescale:

#### Mechanism 1: Showroom Default (immediate)

Every Excitech showroom in Tashkent has at least one demo machine on the floor. You arrange for the demo machine to run from a Mebelchi tablet at all times. Every customer who walks into the showroom sees the software running. The dealer's sales rep doesn't have to say "consider the software" — the software is the demo. Reference Linear's "design as distribution" principle from `-r1_6_community_moat.md`: the artifact in front of the buyer is the marketing.

#### Mechanism 2: Activation Default (90 days post-sale)

When the service engineer commissions the machine at the customer's shop, the software gets installed and a first cut list is run _before the engineer leaves_. Within 90 days, the customer has run 30+ jobs through the software. The sticker traceability play kicks in (per `03_PRODUCT_V1.md` — "once a shop adopts this, switching cost is enormous"). By the time the 12-month free period ends, the shop has 9 months of historical data and physical labels stuck to panels in their warehouse. They renew because de-installing is more expensive than renewing.

#### Mechanism 3: Replacement Default (3–5 year horizon)

The shop's next machine purchase, 5 years later, is anchored to the software. If they're shopping a second machine, they want one that runs the software they already use. This creates a pull-through effect: dealers who don't bundle Mebelchi lose business to dealers who do. By year 4 in your timeline, this is the actual moat — not just the dealer relationship.

This is also why exclusivity-light agreements are dangerous. A 5-year exclusive contract with one dealer locks you out of other dealers in the same market. A _12-month preferred-partner_ agreement with a renewable option is the right structure.

## (e) How SaaS companies have lost dealer channels (and recovered, or not)

Six failure modes recur in channel-conflict literature. Each maps to a specific risk in your model.

### Failure mode 1: Direct sales undercut the dealer

The vendor (you) closes a deal with a customer the dealer was working. Per CompTIA research cited in channel literature, **60% of partners will de-emphasize a vendor due to persistent conflict, and 33% will drop the relationship entirely.**

**How you avoid it:** Deal-registration system. When the dealer demos to a customer, they register that customer's shop name in a shared Google Sheet. For 90 days, that shop is the dealer's account. Even if the shop signs up via your Telegram channel, the dealer gets the spiff. This is the single most important rule in the entire program.

**Recovery if it happens:** Pay the dealer the full spiff _as if they closed it themselves_, even retroactively. Email the dealer's owner with an apology. The relationship will survive one incident; it won't survive a pattern.

### Failure mode 2: Pricing leaks across channels

The customer discovers the software is $9/month via Telegram, but the dealer was charging $15/month bundled into the machine quote. The customer feels betrayed. The dealer feels betrayed. Everybody loses.

**How you avoid it:** Single public price. $15/month everywhere, with the $9/month tier as a clearly-defined "Founding Customer" cohort cap (first 500 shops total, not per-dealer). The dealer doesn't sell a different price — they sell _12 months free, then $15/month at month 13_.

### Failure mode 3: The Chinese OEM builds it themselves

The single highest-risk failure mode in your specific situation. Chinese CNC OEMs (Excitech, KDT, Nanxing) have internal software teams. If they see Mebelchi succeeding at scale, the natural play is for them to clone it.

**How you avoid it:** Your moats per `09_QA_PLAYBOOK.md` (sticker-driven traceability, supplier data network, Telegram-native distribution) are not easily cloned by a Chinese hardware OEM whose core competency is mechanical engineering, not consumer mobile software in Russian/Uzbek. But the _defensive move_ is to be valuable enough to the OEM that they'd rather partner than build. That means hitting 500+ Excitech installs in year 1 — at which point Excitech sees Mebelchi as a sales differentiator, not a cost center to absorb.

**The lesson from Eman in `01_STRATEGY.md`** ("Eman won't build the tool that destroys their factory revenue") applies inversely here: an OEM _will_ build the tool if it captures revenue they don't currently capture. Make sure your revenue is _additive_ to theirs, not substitutive.

### Failure mode 4: The dealer's salesperson never actually sells it

Most common failure mode in the wild. You sign the partnership, run training, send the cards, and 90 days later nothing has happened. The sales reps were polite in the training session but never adopted the new pitch.

**How you avoid it:** The Quarterly Lunch + Update mechanism above, plus a leaderboard. Each rep's activation count is public to the other reps at the same dealer. Reps with zero activations after 60 days get a personal call from the dealer's owner — which only happens if your relationship with the dealer's owner is strong enough that you can ask for it.

**Recovery if it happens:** Replace the dealer. The dealer's owner committed to a number; if the reps aren't hitting it, the owner needs to either change the comp structure or find different reps. Sometimes the right answer is to end the relationship within 6 months rather than letting it drag.

### Failure mode 5: The free-12-months cliff destroys retention

Customer gets the software free with the machine, uses it for 12 months, then gets the $15/month renewal email and churns immediately. You've spent $100–$200 on a SPIFF for nothing.

**How you avoid it:** Two-stage onboarding gates the customer into paid behavior before month 12.

- **Months 1–3:** Activation. Customer must run 30+ cut lists and stick at least 100 QR labels on panels.
- **Months 4–9:** Adoption. The materials catalog gets linked to supplier accounts (per `-r1_2_partnership.md`'s mechanism). The customer is now ordering materials through Mebelchi-generated POs.
- **Months 10–12:** Renewal motion. Automated email + WhatsApp from month 10 onward: "your 12-month preview ends in 60 days. Renew now at the Founding Customer rate of $9/month locked forever, or wait and pay $15/month at month 13." Founding cohort lock is the carrot.

If customer hasn't completed activation by month 6, your customer success person (or the dealer's service engineer, see Mechanism 4 above) intervenes physically.

### Failure mode 6: Two dealers in the same zone fight

You sign Excitech's Tashkent distributor in March. In June, KDT's Tashkent distributor asks to sign. Both want exclusive Tashkent territory. You can't grant it without breaking the first relationship.

**How you avoid it:** Never grant geographic exclusivity. Grant _brand exclusivity_ — Excitech distributor gets exclusive rights to bundle Mebelchi with _Excitech machines_ in Tashkent. KDT distributor gets the same for KDT machines. Both are real exclusivities. Neither competes directly with the other. This is the structure most CAD/CAM vendors use because every shop's CNC brand is fixed.

## 90-Day Pilot Playbook — One Chinese CNC Distributor in Tashkent

This is what you actually do, week by week. Designed around signing one anchor distributor (most likely the local Excitech reseller given their ~30% market share per `02_MARKET_AND_MACHINES.md`, though KDT or Nanxing work equally) and proving the model before generalizing.

### Pre-conditions (must be true before day 1)

- Mebelchi V1 must generate DXF that runs cleanly on the candidate distributor's most-sold machine model. Validated by a real cut list on a real machine, in front of the dealer.
- You must have a named champion inside the distributor — ideally the sales director or the owner. Per `-r1_1_distribution.md`, blue-collar B2B sales runs on relationships, not cold outreach. Use the factory friend's network from `01_STRATEGY.md`.
- You need the printed pocket cards, demo tablet, video, ROI spreadsheet, personalized URLs, and a Telegram group ready to deploy on day 8.

### Phase 1 (Days 1–14): Discovery + commitment

**Days 1–3:** Schedule a sit-down with the distributor's owner. Not the sales rep — the owner. Bring the factory friend. Bring a printed copy of the case-study summary above. Show the demo on his showroom machine.

**Day 4–7:** Send a one-page partnership proposal. Single number on the cover page: _"We expect to deliver an additional 8–12 machine sales to you in the next 12 months from your customers asking 'and which dealer carries Mebelchi?'"_ The frame is "you sell more machines because of us," not "you sell our software."

**Days 8–10:** Negotiate the agreement. Key terms:

- 12-month bundle: every machine sold gets 12 months of free Mebelchi access.
- $100 SPIFF per activation to the rep, $50 month-13 retention bonus.
- 20% recurring commission to the dealer entity, months 13–24.
- No exclusivity yet. Both sides can walk after 90 days.
- Deal registration on a shared Google Sheet. 90-day window.

**Days 11–14:** Sign the agreement. Onboard the dealer's CRM (if they have one). Set up the Telegram channel for the dealer's sales team. Schedule first training session for day 17.

### Phase 2 (Days 15–30): Train + seed

**Days 15–16:** Print the cards, prep the tablets, finalize the videos. Sync personalized URLs to each rep.

**Day 17:** First training session. 90 minutes max. Format:

- 10 min: founder intro + the ROI math (Oppoq + factory friend in person)
- 30 min: live demo on the showroom machine — actual cut list, actual cut, actual QR-labeled panels
- 20 min: pocket card walkthrough, ROI spreadsheet, where the spiff lands in their pay
- 30 min: Q&A and complaints

End with lunch. Each rep walks out with: 50 cards, the personalized URL on their phone, the WhatsApp group joined.

**Days 18–22:** First showroom installation. The dealer's most-trafficked showroom gets a permanent Mebelchi tablet on the demo machine. Loaded with sample data from real Tashkent kitchens. Always-on, always-running, integrated into the standard machine demo script.

**Days 23–30:** Shadow the first 3–5 customer demos. Founder or factory friend physically present at the dealer's showroom for the first week of post-training activity. Take notes on what the reps say wrong. Update the cards within 7 days based on observed friction.

### Phase 3 (Days 31–60): Activation + iteration

**Days 31–45:** First wave of activated installs. Target: 5–10 customer installations in the first 30 days of selling. Each install happens via the service engineer at the customer's shop, with you (or factory friend) present for at least 2 of them to refine the install playbook.

**Days 46–60:** Weekly partner scorecard.

|Week|Sales rep installs|Customer activations (7-day)|Spiffs paid|Churn (90-day cohort)|
|---|---|---|---|---|
|Week 5|N|M|$X|N/A|
|Week 6|...|...|...|...|

This dashboard is shared with the dealer's owner every Monday at 9am. The dealer's owner sees the same numbers you see. If activations are low, the dealer's owner — not you — calls the reps.

Run the first Quarterly Lunch on day 60. Bring kebab. Hand out updated pocket cards. Demo any new features.

### Phase 4 (Days 61–90): Validate the unit economics

By day 90 you should have data on:

- **Machine sales attributed to the partnership:** raw count of machines sold by the dealer with the bundle active
- **Activation rate:** % of bundled machines where the software is actually installed within 30 days. Target: 70%+ (low bar — the bundle is free, so anyone who declines is opting out actively)
- **First-cut-list rate:** % of activated installs that run a real cut list within 7 days. Target: 50%+. Below 30% means the install playbook is broken.
- **Cost per activated install:** SPIFF + your operational cost (engineer time, tablet, demos). Target: under $150.
- **Spiff dispute rate:** how often the dealer's rep claims a SPIFF you didn't validate. Target: <5%.

By day 90 you have a yes/no decision. The two scenarios:

**Scenario A (signal):** 8+ machines sold with bundle, 60%+ activation rate, no major channel-conflict incidents. Sign a second dealer (different brand) in week 13. Expand to Samarkand by month 6.

**Scenario B (no signal):** <5 machines sold, activation <40%. Don't sign more dealers. Diagnose: is the dealer pushing it? Is the rep comp wrong? Is the customer not interested? Iterate inside this single relationship for another 60 days before adding complexity.

## Leading indicators to track weekly (the channel scorecard)

Add to your weekly metric dashboard alongside the supplier and Telegram scorecards. Per-partner:

|Metric|Calculation|Target by Day 90|Red flag|
|---|---|---|---|
|Machines sold with bundle|Count, 7-day rolling|≥ 2/week|< 1/week for 3 weeks|
|Bundle acceptance rate|% of new machine sales with bundle active|≥ 85%|< 60%|
|Time-to-install|Median days from machine delivery to first software login|≤ 7 days|> 21 days|
|First-cut-list completion|% of installs running a real cut list in week 1|≥ 50%|< 30%|
|Rep activation per rep|Installs/rep/month|≥ 1.5|0 for any single rep for 2 months|
|Spiff payout latency|Days from activation to spiff payment|≤ 45 days|> 60 days|
|Month-13 conversion (lagging)|% of bundled customers who pay at month 13|≥ 35%|< 20%|
|Dealer NPS (quarterly call)|Owner's rating 0–10|≥ 7|≤ 5|
|Channel conflict incidents|Count of customer/dealer/Mebelchi attribution disputes|0|≥ 2|

The metric I'd most watch in the first 90 days is **rep activation per rep**. If 2 reps out of 6 are doing all the installs, you have a champion problem — those 2 reps are the entire dealer relationship in disguise. If those 2 reps leave, the dealer relationship dies overnight. The fix is to identify the structural reason the other 4 aren't selling (often: the demo is too time-consuming) and either fix it or write off the 4 and concentrate compensation on the 2.

## The iron rule

The dealer channel is your highest-LTV channel but your slowest-to-activate channel. From `-r1_5_revenue.md`, your year-one mix is roughly 700 supplier-referred, 200 field-visit, 100 Telegram. The dealer channel doesn't appear in that mix because it's not material in year one — it's the year-two and year-three story.

The bet you're making is: **by year three, when you have 2,500 paid shops and $30K MRR, 30–40% of your new logos will come from CNC dealers**. That's only true if you start the partnership work _now_, accept that the first 6 months will produce maybe 30 customers, and refuse to abandon the channel because supplier referrals look so much better in year one.

Romchi's parallel on the window side took roughly 24 months to produce meaningful dealer-bundled distribution. You should expect the same timeline. Plant in month 4. Harvest in month 24.
