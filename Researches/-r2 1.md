# (a) The commercial structures that actually get adopted (with typical %s / $ mechanics)

Below are the channel deal patterns that show up repeatedly in industrial + CAD/CAM ecosystems, and _how they map to a low-ARPU ($9–15/mo) CNC-file SaaS_.

## 1) **Reseller margin (wholesale → retail)**

**Structure**

- Dealer “buys” subscriptions at a **wholesale discount** and resells at MSRP, owning billing and first-line sales.

**Typical economics (common in B2B channel programs)**

- Software-only reseller margins in practice often land in the **20–40%** range _when the partner is expected to do real selling_, and then partners try to add services margin on top (implementation/training). (Not every vendor publishes the exact % publicly; the more “hands-on” you make the partner motion, the higher the margin they demand.)

**Why it’s hard at $9–15/mo**

- Even **30%** of $12/mo is **$3.60/mo**—not enough for a dealer rep to care _unless_ it is packaged with paid commissioning/training.

**What makes it work**

- Force the “real money” into **dealer services attach** (see #4) and keep subscription margin as an annuity.

**Evidence pattern**

- The SolidWorks reseller ecosystem explicitly frames itself as VAR-led, and VAR businesses describe their model as a blend of **licenses + service** (i.e., the service attach is not optional—it’s the profit engine). [1](https://blogs.solidworks.com/solidworksblog/2014/04/how-do-i-become-a-solidworks-reseller.html)

---

## 2) **Referral / lead fee (dealer introduces, you close & bill)**

**Structure**

- Dealer registers a lead; you close; you pay a commission.

**Typical economics**

- **10–20% of first-year ARR** (or **1–3 months of subscription**) is common in SaaS referral programs, sometimes paid only after the customer stays ≥60–90 days.

**When it works**

- When the dealer sales team is weak on software selling but strong on access and trust.

**Key operational add-on: Deal registration**

- A formal **deal registration** process exists specifically to (a) credit the partner, (b) reduce channel conflict, and (c) justify partner support from the vendor. [5](https://www.techtarget.com/searchitchannel/definition/deal-registration)

---

## 3) **Quarterly target incentives + rebates (classic industrial channel lever)**

Instead of “% per deal,” many vendors pay **bonuses for hitting targets**.

**Structure**

- Dealer gets: base margin + **quarterly attainment reward** + possibly **performance rebates**.

**Concrete public evidence**

- Autodesk discloses a **partner incentive program** that uses **quarterly attainment of monetary rewards** for distributors/resellers to hit mutually agreed goals. [2](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/769397/000076939725000019/adsk-20250131.htm)
- Markforged discloses that some VAR contracts include **performance rebates** tied to sales targets (accounted for as variable consideration reducing revenue). [4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1816613/000095017025046869/mkfg-20241231.htm)

**Why it matters for you**

- With low subscription dollars, you can’t “fund motivation” purely with per-seat margin. A quarterly bonus tied to _machines sold with your software activated_ is often more behavior-shaping than a tiny recurring cut.

---

## 4) **Dealer services attach (commissioning, training, post-processor setup)**

This is the **make-or-break** lever for $9–15/mo products.

**Structure**

- Dealer sells a **one-time paid package** at machine delivery:
    - “CNC Software Setup & First Job Pack”
    - includes: post-processor selection, tool library template, first cut verification, and 2–4 hours training
- Subscription remains low-cost, but dealer makes real margin upfront.

**Concrete evidence that VARs live on services**

- A VAR business-model slide deck from Visiativ (a long-time SolidWorks VAR) describes the VAR model as **70% licenses / 30% service** for SolidWorks. [6](https://www.visiativ.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eng-visiativ-annual-results-slideshow-2016-1.pdf)

**Typical dollars that tend to clear in industrial CNC contexts**

- Dealers routinely charge **hundreds to a few thousand USD** for commissioning/training in many industrial categories (varies heavily by country and machine complexity). For your target markets, you’d tune this to local willingness-to-pay—often by anchoring it against “lost sheet goods + downtime” rather than software value.

---

## 5) **Bundled-at-point-of-sale (POS) inclusion (software is “free with the machine”)**

**Structure**

- Machine quote includes software as included value; customer perceives it as default.
- Dealer/OEM pays vendor (you) via:
    - per-machine royalty, or
    - prepaid X-month subscription per machine, or
    - a bulk annual commit.

**Concrete public examples (wood/CNC adjacent)**

- ShopBot includes **Vectric VCarve Pro** with every new ShopBot purchase and explicitly prices it as **$0** with a **$699 value**. [7](https://shopbottools.com/products/software/)
- SigmaNEST publicly lists machine OEM relationships and even calls itself the **preferred nesting software** for specific machine partners (e.g., “SigmaNEST for Alltra Machines”). [8](https://www.sigmanest.com/en/machine-partners)

**Your key economic trick**

- Bundle **a fixed included term** (e.g., 6–12 months) funded by:
    - a small increase in machine package price, or
    - a commissioning package fee.
- Then convert to renewal with dealer still getting credit.

---

## 6) **Co-marketing / MDF (Market Development Funds)**

**Structure**

- Vendor allocates funds that partners can spend on approved marketing (events, webinars, local ads), often as a **% of their sales**.

**Typical percentages (industry norms)**

- Definitions and mechanics: vendors grant MDF to channel partners to support selling activities; often based on a **percentage of partner sales**. [9](https://www.techtarget.com/searchitchannel/definition/market-development-funds-MDF)
- Typical allocation often cited as **~2–5% of channel revenue** in mature partner operations (varies by sector), and some sources cite **~1–2% of top-line revenue** allocated as MDF by vendors. [10](https://www.magentrix.com/glossary/market-development-funds-mdf)

**What MDF should mean for you (practically)**

- In your case, MDF isn’t “brand marketing.” It should fund:
    - demo days at the dealer showroom,
    - Uzbek/Russian/Turkish-language short workshops,
    - travel for an applications engineer to do 3 joint installs.

# (c) Training a CNC dealer sales team to pitch SaaS: what works vs. what fails

Industrial dealers are good at:

- machine specs,
- financing,
- installation,
- service contracts.

They are usually **not** good at:

- demonstrating software value quickly,
- handling onboarding friction,
- selling “small recurring.”

Public filings explicitly warn that **recruiting and training** VARs takes “significant time and resources.” [4](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1816613/000095017025046869/mkfg-20241231.htm)

## What works (in the field)

### 1) Train to a **10-minute demo** + 3 outcomes (not features)

Dealer reps need a script like:

1. Import cabinet/furniture dimensions →
2. Generate cut list + nesting →
3. Export **DXF/CSV/MPR/CIX** →
4. “First sheet cut today.”

Your SaaS wins if the rep can promise _time-to-first-cut_ rather than “software capabilities.”

### 2) Make certification **role-based**

Split enablement into:

- **Sales** (positioning, qualifying, pricing, objections)
- **Applications** (posts, tool libraries, controller quirks)
- **Service** (what to do when output doesn’t match)

### 3) Pay spiffs on the behavior you need

If you want “bundled by default,” pay for:

- **activation within 14 days of machine install**, not “a subscription sold.”

### 4) Give them a **commissioning checklist** that includes your SaaS

Dealers already run checklists for:

- leveling,
- spindle warmup,
- vacuum test,
- tool calibration.

Add:

- “software installed + first job exported + first cut verified.”

### 5) Keep support boundaries brutally clear

A channel fails when the dealer becomes your unpaid helpdesk. Do:

- dealer = first-line “how to buy + basics”
- you = in-app support, posts, file correctness issues

MachineMetrics (industrial machine monitoring SaaS) markets a partner network that includes **training and support** for partners—because partners won’t sell what they can’t support. [13](https://www.machinemetrics.com/partner-program)

## What fails (predictably)

1. **Long trainings** (multi-day) before the first deal
2. No demo environment (rep can’t show value live)
3. No protection: partner brings a lead, vendor closes direct (channel conflict)
4. Misaligned incentives: dealer only gets paid once, but is expected to support renewals forever
5. Product complexity that requires CNC-programmer-level expertise to get a first success

Use **deal registration** to reduce fear and protect partner effort. [5](https://www.techtarget.com/searchitchannel/definition/deal-registration)

# (d) Making your SaaS the “default bundle” without illegal exclusivity / tying problems

You asked specifically about “default at every machine sale” _without violating exclusivity laws_. The safe approach is: **preferred-by-default, not forced-by-contract**, with optionality and clean pricing.

## The legal risk in plain language

- **Tying** (forcing purchase of Product B to get Product A) can be illegal in some circumstances—especially if the seller has sufficient market power in the tying product. [3](https://www.ftc.gov/advice-guidance/competition-guidance/guide-antitrust-laws/single-firm-conduct/tying-sale-two-products)
- In EU-style vertical frameworks, market-share thresholds (often **30%**) affect whether a vertical agreement is block-exempt, and restrictions like exclusive purchasing/single branding can become sensitive. [14](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=legissum%3A4609819)
- Turkey’s competition framework explicitly treats **product tying** as an example of potentially abusive conduct by a dominant firm (and Turkish practice discusses tying/bundling in dominance contexts). [15](https://www.nortonrosefulbright.com/en/knowledge/publications/5ca74444/doing-business-in-turkey-competition-law)

## Practical compliance-oriented pattern: “Preferred bundle, optional line item”

To make “default” stick while staying non-exclusive:

1. **Quote structure**

- Machine price (line item)
- “Software setup & first-job pack” (line item)
- Subscription (line item, cancellable)

2. **Customer choice**

- Customer can decline subscription (or switch later)
- Dealer still gets paid for commissioning work they actually did.

3. **Non-exclusive dealer contract**

- No clause that forbids other software.
- Instead: incentives for _activations, renewals, low churn, low support burden._

4. **Technical openness**

- Publish export specs, APIs, or at minimum “works with…” matrix
- Avoid technical lock-in that looks like exclusion.

5. **No retaliatory terms**

- Don’t punish a dealer for selling a competing CAD/CAM; just reward the behavior you want.

# The Honest Mechanics of Award → Customer Conversion

Before the playbook, one truth most award-marketing content obscures: an award alone almost never converts to customers. What it converts to is **permission to be taken seriously by people who would otherwise have ignored your cold email.** The conversion happens in the next step, which is where most founders fumble.

## (a) Award → Paid Customers: What Actually Happens

The cleanest documented case is Dropbox's TechCrunch50 2008 launch — the most-studied "award catapulted a company" story in tech. Looking at the actual numbers, though, the story is more nuanced than the legend. In summer 2008 Drew Houston's team "invested in a big launch event at TechCrunch50, hired a PR firm, launched an affiliate program, and began to buy AdWords", but by early 2009 "none of its traditional digital strategies were driving acquisitions at an acceptable cost. Obvious keywords were too expensive to make sense for the startup, and long tail search wasn't providing the type of volume it needed. Display ads and affiliate didn't work out either, as the overall CPA across traditional digital channels hovered between $233 – $388 [for a $99 product]". Dropbox went from 100,000 registered users in September 2008 to 4,000,000 by January 2010 — but "Mostly from word-of-mouth and viral: 35% of daily signups from referral program, 20% from shared folders, other viral features."

The TechCrunch50 win mattered, but as **a stage, not a funnel.** It gave Dropbox the trust marker that made the referral mechanic credible to invited users. The product did the conversion work; the award unlocked the audience that could feel the product. For Mebelchi this matters enormously: small furniture workshops do not have viral coefficients. You need a different conversion path — one based on trust transfer through credible intermediaries (banks, the Ministry of Digital Technologies, IT Park, Mebel-Expo Tashkent), not on a viral loop that doesn't exist in B2B vertical SaaS for fragmented manufacturing.

The clearer modern case is **PryvX**, a cybersecurity startup that won the EU Startups Investment Pitch Competition. Within a year of starting, PryvX "signed a strategic partnership agreement with INDICO by Telkomsel, the digital subsidiary of Telkomsel, Indonesia's largest telecom operator. This is on the back of the new Indonesian Personal Data Protection Law that went into effect on 17 October 2024. They also have pilots running with BT Group and 3 Sweden." The pattern: award win + regulatory tailwind + named enterprise validators. PryvX didn't get those Telkomsel and BT contracts because they won an award — they got them because the award made the cold outreach answerable. That is the realistic conversion rate to expect: not "award → customers," but "award → reply rate jumps from ~2% to ~20% on outreach to decision-makers who matter."

## (c) Turning One Win Into 12 Months of Compounding Press

The cycle that disciplined B2B PR teams run looks roughly like this — and yes, it gives you a steady drumbeat without any single moment being "fake news":

**Month 0 (award week):** Win announcement, founder quote in the press release the organizer issues. Don't write your own — get quoted in theirs. **Month 1:** Your own announcement (longer, with customer testimonial), distributed to trade press (the Uzbek/Russian-language ones plus furniture-industry English-language outlets like FDM Asia, Woodworking Network). **Month 2–3:** Founder bylined op-ed in a national outlet about _the industry problem you solve_ — not about your company. This is the Tarman principle: "focus less on 'communications for communication's sake' and more on aligning messaging with the organization's development stage", and the framework operates on 3, 6, 12-month horizons. **Month 4:** First customer case study, distributed to one tier-1 trade publication exclusively first, then to general press a week later. **Month 5–6:** Original research / data report on the state of your industry (e.g., "State of Uzbek Furniture Manufacturing 2027" — a survey of 200 workshops). Journalists love unique data. "74% [of journalists] want to receive press releases from PR pros, and 61% want original research reports with trends and market data." **Month 7–8:** Customer milestone post (100 workshops, 1,000 jobs cut, whatever metric is real). Tie to a national policy moment. **Month 9:** Newsjacking — your founder commentary on a relevant policy event. "The optimal newsjacking window is typically within 30 minutes to 2 hours after a significant story breaks, when journalists are still developing coverage and seeking expert commentary." **Month 10–11:** Sequel award / international recognition (Startup Wheel International Track, AppsAfrica, Seedstars, MIT TR35 EMEA, etc.). Re-references the original PTA win as authority. **Month 12:** One-year retrospective post (your own channels) feeding into next-cycle PTA application as a "where are they now" story.

Realistic budget for an emerging-market vertical SaaS: "Series A PR should cost $5K-8K/month for agency or $8K-12K for in-house + tools" — at your stage, $1.5K–3K/month with a freelance PR person in Tashkent or Almaty is achievable. The honest baseline expectation: "First placements: 6-10 weeks from PR program start... Pipeline attribution: 20-28 weeks", and you should expect leading indicators (inbound inquiries from banks, government, suppliers) in 90 days, not in week one.

## (d) Leveraging Judges and Sponsors as Channel Partners

This is the part most founders waste. The judges and sponsors are pre-screened bag-carriers for your distribution — but only if you treat them as future customers/partners, not as benevolent ecosystem actors.

For the President Tech Award specifically, the sponsor stack is **Ministry of Digital Technologies + IT Park Uzbekistan + Plug and Play + state banks (Uzpromstroybank, Asaka Bank, NBU and others rotate as ecosystem sponsors)**. Each one is a separate channel partner that opens a different door.

2. **Bring a one-pager** that frames Mebelchi as _their_ success story: "Here's the joint case study we can publish in Q1." Banks and ministries are starved for tangible startup-impact stories they can point to in their own annual reports — give them yours, branded with theirs.
3. **Ask for one specific thing** per partner. Bank: a meeting with their SME lending team to discuss whether Mebelchi's part-traceability data could feed inventory-based working capital lending. Ministry: a letter of support for a furniture-industry pilot. The award is the introduction; the contracts are the score. ## (e) The Playbook for Winning a Non-Glamorous Micro-SaaS Award

The advantage of furniture manufacturing software is structural: when 90% of the applicant pool is AI wrappers and consumer apps, a real B2B product with real revenue is rare enough to stand out. The 2025 PTA Micro-SaaS & AdTech winner was Oygul, a student team from New Uzbekistan University. The 2025 hackathon winner was Stack Overflowers' StreetScreens (DOOH ad marketplace) — also abstract/digital. There is no precedent in the category for a deeply industrial, locally-grounded vertical SaaS, which is exactly why one should win.

Concrete tactics:

**Quantify the boring stuff aggressively.** The PTA jury is increasingly international (the 2025 final featured external experts and Plug and Play partners) and they read English. Lead with: total furniture workshops in Uzbekistan (~6,000+ by industry estimates), average waste rate from manual cutting plans (typically 15–25% of sheet material), value of that waste at current MDF/chipboard prices, and the share of workshops with no software at all (close to 100%). The TAM math should be on slide three.

**Show locked customer logos, not just letters of intent.** If you have even five workshops paying anything — even at a steep early-adopter discount — that beats every "we have signed 40 LOIs" deck. Procurement officers and judges respond to revenue. The award organizers will quietly verify revenue claims against IT Park resident filings, so don't inflate.

**Lead with traceability and compliance angle, not the productivity angle.** Productivity software is everywhere; compliance and traceability is the wedge that gets you into the Ministry's regulatory pilot conversation. Mebelchi's thermal label / part traceability play is the strongest narrative hook because it ties to the same trust-and-verification framework the country is building under YP-11 and the Xolis digital wallet rollout. Frame Mebelchi as "the part-traceability infrastructure layer for Uzbekistan's largest manufacturing employer."

**Build the demo around one workshop owner's actual day.** Avoid the generic "AI-powered platform" pitch. Show a real Tashkent-region workshop owner — name, photo, monthly material savings in soums — and walk through one shift on Mebelchi. The jury sits through 24 pitches in a day; the one that doesn't sound like ChatGPT generated it is the one they remember at deliberation.

**Pre-wire the room.** Before finals, have at least three named industry references (one big bank, one Mebel-Expo organizer, one large workshop) willing to take a phone call from any judge who asks. Judges actually call references on the high-scoring entries. Almost no other team does this.

# r1_7 — Viral Referral Mechanics

**Question:** How do we engineer Romchi-style $0-marketing organic growth for Mebelchi — with the specific Telegram, mahalla, and cash-economy mechanics our market actually runs on?

**Short answer:** B2B SaaS viral coefficient (K-factor) over 1.0 is essentially mythological — Slack and Dropbox are the outliers, not the playbook. Real B2B benchmarks are K = 0.2–0.7, with 0.2 already considered good. Romchi's "$0 marketing growth" was not technically viral by this definition; it was structurally cheap acquisition through three channels (Telegram cluster diffusion, supplier pushes, professional reputation forwarding) that produced low blended CAC, not exponential self-sustaining growth.

For Mebelchi at $9/month ARPU, the math forces a specific design: we cannot pay $20 cash per referral (that's two months of revenue from the referrer), we cannot afford referral fraud at scale, and we cannot use email/SMS attribution because our payment rails (Click, Payme, cash) don't connect cleanly to acquisition source. The design that works is **public status mechanics + Telegram deep-linked attribution + "1 month free" as the only cash-equivalent reward**, with the referral mechanic baked into the product output (the quote PDF, the cut map, the sticker) rather than bolted on as a separate feature.

This document is the case-study analysis, the math, the Telegram-specific mechanics, the attribution architecture, and a 60-day rollout plan with specific UI surfaces described.

## 1. What viral B2B actually looks like in blue-collar trade software

Five companies, ranked by structural relevance to us. None of them are pure viral plays. All of them mix referral with what's really a distribution loop — supplier pushes, community status, output sharing, or paid trainers.

### 1.1 ServiceTitan — viral through the Certified Provider ecosystem (★★★★★)

**The structural play:** ServiceTitan is the dominant SaaS for home service contractors. They do not run a customer-facing "refer a friend, get $X" program in the traditional sense. Instead they built a three-layer distribution motion:

- **Certified User Program** (free, role-based, three sub-badges before "Certified User" status) — turns existing customers into internal champions
- **Certified Provider Program** — independent consultants (Powerhouse, BDR, Titan Pro, Go Time, Titanium-tier partners) who get paid by both ServiceTitan and contractors. They prospect and sell ServiceTitan as part of their consulting business.
- **App Marketplace** — third-party software vendors get certified to integrate, creating a flywheel of vendors who actively market ServiceTitan to their customers as the "platform you should run."

ServiceTitan does not publish viral coefficient numbers. But the certified-provider channel is now substantial — multiple Titanium-tier partners ($500K+/year ServiceTitan revenue from referrals).

**What we steal:** The recognition that paid certified trainers are a better referral mechanism than $20 cash bounties — they create a professional class with skin in the game.

### 1.2 Housecall Pro — the affiliate model with real economics (★★★★☆)

**The structural play:** Housecall Pro runs an affiliate/partner program paying **up to $1,000 per referral that signs up**, plus a customized co-branded landing page and exposure to the 35,000+ Pro community.

**The economics:** Their average customer pays roughly $150–300/month and has long retention. $1,000 represents ~5–6 months of revenue. The math works because LTV is high enough to absorb it, and the affiliates are typically other business owners (consultants, equipment dealers, industry media) who refer multiple customers and don't break the program.

**Why this doesn't translate to us:** At $9–15/month ARPU, $1,000 per referral is 67–111 months of revenue. The same model at our pricing would need to pay roughly **$30–60 per referral** to be proportional, and even that is brutal at our LTV ($300 target). The right Housecall Pro learning is _the structure_: separate affiliate program for partners (suppliers, dealers, industry players) from customer-to-customer referrals.

### 1.3 Procore — referral built around the customer success layer (★★★☆☆)

**The structural play:** Procore's customer success function is the actual viral engine — not a referral program. They embedded their software into construction PM workflows so deeply that switching becomes painful for both the company and its trade contractor subcontractors, who get pulled in for free as collaborators. Every general contractor using Procore drags 5–20 sub-contractors onto the platform — those sub-contractors then become candidates for paid Procore accounts at their next project.

**The viral mechanism is the workflow.** Not a coupon, not a badge — the fact that the GC requires the sub to use Procore for the project.

**What we steal:** **Workflow-embedded virality** — every quote PDF the mebelchi sends to his customer is signed by Mebelchi at the bottom. Every sticker scanned by another shop's operator is a Mebelchi sticker. Every customer who orders a kitchen sees the brand. This is far cheaper than any cash incentive.

### 1.4 Jobber — the "Referrals" feature as a paid add-on (★★☆☆☆)

**The structural play:** Jobber has a Referrals feature, but interestingly it is a **paid add-on to most plans** — not the headline acquisition channel. With 350,000+ users, Jobber's actual growth comes from CMO-led brand marketing, content marketing, and industry partnerships. The Referrals feature is a customer-side tool (a contractor uses it to track _their_ customer referrals for their cleaning/HVAC business), not the way Jobber acquires customers.

**Why this matters:** It's a useful counterexample. Building a polished "refer a friend" feature in V1 is the wrong instinct. Jobber's growth came from elsewhere, and they monetize the referral feature by selling it back to customers.

**What we steal:** Don't build a fancy referral product feature. Build the cheapest possible attribution loop in V1, save engineering for the core product.

### 1.5 Lincoln Electric distributor "Honors Program" — the partner training viral loop (★★★★☆)

From r1_6: Lincoln Electric trains distributors through a 4-level Honors Program, with online + in-person components. Distributors who get certified become better sellers (and more committed promoters) of Lincoln gear vs Miller/Hobart/ESAB. This is the supplier-side equivalent of ServiceTitan's Certified Provider — a multi-decade compounding effect of trained channel partners.

**What we steal:** Combine the certification track from r1_6 with the supplier-partner track from r1_2. Trained suppliers refer more.

## 2. The math: what Romchi's "$0 marketing" actually was, and what we need to hit

This is the part most founders get wrong. "Viral" is a vague word; the math is unforgiving.

### 2.1 The viral coefficient formula and benchmarks

The standard formula: **K = i × c**

- i = average number of invitations sent per user
- c = average conversion rate of those invitations

K-factor benchmarks for context:

- B2B SaaS average K-factor is around 0.2; over 0.2 is "quite good"; 0.3–0.7 is "strong for B2B"
- K > 1.0 produces exponential, self-sustaining growth; most B2B SaaS operates at 0.15–0.7
- B2B viral cycle times are typically 8–12 weeks (vs 1–3 days for consumer)
- A K-factor of 0.5 means every 100 acquired users eventually become ~200 (1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.125 + ...) — a 2× multiplier on every dollar spent on acquisition
- According to Pacific Crest research, enterprise SaaS sees lower viral coefficients than consumer SaaS but offsets with higher LTV
- B2B SaaS companies have the highest hill to climb, where 99.999% won't reach K > 1

**The hard truth:** Mebelchi is unlikely to ever achieve K > 1. That's fine. **Our realistic target is K = 0.4–0.6**, which is the upper-middle range for B2B SaaS and matches the "blue-collar trade-cluster" structural advantage we have.

### 2.2 What K = 0.5 looks like in our funnel

Working backward from the 1,000-paying-shop target in r1_5:

|Input|Value|Source|
|---|---|---|
|Paying customers|1,000|r1_5 target|
|% who refer at least once|30%|Realistic in dense trade clusters|
|Number of active referrers|300||
|Average referrals sent per referrer|4|Telegram forwards, in-person mentions|
|Total referrals sent|1,200||
|Trial conversion of referrals|50%|Warmer than cold leads (vs 12% Telegram cold in r1_5)|
|Trial signups from referrals|600||
|Trial → paid conversion|50%|Higher than blended 35% (warm intro)|
|Paid customers from referrals|300||
|**K factor**|**0.30**|Conservative case|

A more optimistic case with 40% of customers referring (the certification-enrolled cohort retains better and refers more):

|Input|Value|
|---|---|
|Active referrers|400 (40%)|
|Avg referrals sent|5|
|Total referrals|2,000|
|Trial conversion|50%|
|Trial signups|1,000|
|Trial → paid|55% (mahalla-warmth boost)|
|Paid from referrals|550|
|**K factor**|**0.55**|

This is the operating band. **Conservative K = 0.30, target K = 0.55**. Both are below 1.0, both substantially reduce blended CAC.

### 2.3 What Romchi's growth really was

The Romchi $25K MRR ramp over 18 months was not viral in the K > 1 sense. Decomposing what we know from `01_STRATEGY.md` and `09_QA_PLAYBOOK.md` and conversations with the Romchi CTO mentor:

- **Telegram-clustered organic distribution** = ~K of 0.3–0.5 estimated (matches the math above)
- **Supplier-led acquisition** = the highest-leverage channel (r1_5: nearly equal to field visits, with zero field time)
- **Founder authority and content** = compounding inbound from technical reputation in the windows trade

The accurate framing is _low blended CAC_, not _viral_. The Romchi CAC was likely $10–30 per paying customer, not zero — but their LTV was $300+ on a sticky product, so LTV:CAC stayed strongly positive without paid ads.

**This is the model we copy:** not "build a referral program that goes viral" but "build a system where each acquisition channel has CAC under $50 and one of them (supplier) approaches $0 incremental per customer at scale."

## 3. The dark side: why "$20 referral coupons" fail in blue-collar B2B

The standard SaaS referral playbook ("refer a friend, get $20 credit") is wrong for this market. Three forces work against it:

### 3.1 The awkwardness problem

In small trade communities where everyone knows everyone, cash referral bonuses create social friction. From an Alignable forum thread among contractors: _"I've tried that a couple times in the past and it was sometimes a little frustrating, a couple people actually thought they should have received more money than what they received, making the whole situation a little awkward."_

A mebelchi who refers another mebelchi for a $20 credit has to decide: do I tell my friend I'm getting paid? If yes, the friend now feels marketed-to. If no, and the friend finds out, the relationship is poisoned. Either way the cash incentive is a relational liability, not an asset.

### 3.2 The math problem

At $9–15/month ARPU with $300 LTV target, the maximum sustainable referral payment is roughly $30–50 — and that consumes the entire LTV gain from the new customer. Industry data shows referral CAC typically runs 1/3 to 1/2 of paid ads, but at our price point, paid ads aren't viable either, so the comparison breaks. **The right ceiling for cash-equivalent reward at $9/month is one free month (~$9 value)**, paid only after the referee pays for one month themselves.

### 3.3 The motivation problem

The Tesla insight: _"money can't buy"_ rewards (factory tours, exclusive access) outperform cash for status-conscious customers. As Jonah Berger of Wharton noted, the motivation for word of mouth is often self-seeking — gaining social currency by appearing as an expert. A 50-year-old shop owner in Sergeli is not motivated by a $20 credit. He is motivated by:

1. Being the person in the mahalla who knows about the new tool first
2. Public recognition that he is "Tarbiyachi" (Mentor) to other masters
3. Privileged access (founders' calls, early features, supplier discounts)
4. A wall plaque or printed certificate showing rank
5. The ability to convert his mentorship status into more paying customers for _his_ furniture business

This is what the Mebelchi referral program optimizes for, not coupons.

### 3.4 What actually works — the alternative incentive stack

```
PRIMARY (status mechanics):
  Public scoreboard of top referrers in the Mebelchi Telegram group
  Visible Tarbiyachi (Mentor) badge in their app profile
  Promotion track to "Usta" (Level 2) and "Bosh Usta" (Level 3) from r1_6
  Featured placement in "Find a Master" directory

SECONDARY (real economic value, but indirect):
  Supplier discount card (5-15% off LDSP/hardware at partner suppliers)
  Free thermal label printer at 5 successful referrals
  Free tablet at 10 successful referrals
  Annual conference invitation (Mebelchi Day / "Mebelchi Kuni")

TERTIARY (subscription-tied, low cost to us):
  1 month free per successful referral (capped 12/year)
  Annual plan upgrade for top 20 referrers
```

Notice what's missing: **cash**. There is no path in the program to receive a wire transfer from us. This is intentional — it removes the awkwardness, removes the fraud surface, and keeps the program inside the brand's status economy.

## 4. The Telegram-specific viral mechanics

Telegram is not just a chat app for our audience — it is the operating system for the trade. The mechanics below are specific to how mebelchi actually use Telegram, and they multiply referral velocity by 5–10x vs email/SMS.

### 4.1 The forward is the unit of currency, not the link

WhatsApp shares a link. Email shares a link. Telegram **forwards a message** — and crucially, the forwarded message carries the original sender's name as the attribution header. When mebelchi A forwards mebelchi B a message from the Mebelchi bot, B sees:

```
Forwarded from Karim Aka (Bosh Usta, Sergeli mahalla)
  → "Bo'shliq! Yangi DXF chiqdi, oshxonani 10 daqiqada
     hisoblayman. Sinab ko'r: t.me/MebelchiBot?start=ref_karim_847"
     [Open in Mebelchi]
```

Three psychological mechanics fire at once:

1. **Source endorsement** — Karim's name is on it (Telegram does this automatically)
2. **Status display** — "Bosh Usta, Sergeli mahalla" is part of his profile
3. **Frictionless attribution** — the start parameter `ref_karim_847` is encoded in the deep link, with zero extra steps for the referrer

This is invisible to email/SMS and is the single biggest Telegram-specific advantage. Build the system so that the _natural_ sharing motion (forwarding) automatically does the attribution.

### 4.2 Voice messages — the trust transmitter

Mebelchi do not type long messages. They send voice notes. A 22-second voice note from one master to another saying "Brat, sinab ko'r bu dasturni, oshxonani 10 daqiqada chiqarayapti, men 3 oydir foydalanayapman" carries more conviction than any video testimonial we could film.

**Design implication:** the bot's onboarding sequence offers to record a 30-second voice note "for your friend" — `[Tap to record a voice intro you can forward to other masters]`. The voice note is saved as a file in the bot, the user gets a deep link wrapped around it, and they forward both. The referee sees the voice note from someone they know + the auto-generated link.

This is the equivalent of Dropbox's "two-sided free storage" incentive — except the bandwidth is trust, not bytes. No SaaS competitor builds this because western SaaS audiences don't use voice notes the way Russian-speaking trade communities do.

### 4.3 The custom sticker pack — distribution disguised as memes

Sticker culture in Uzbekistan and the CIS is intense. Every Telegram user has dozens of sticker packs installed. **Branded sticker packs are forwarded thousands of times more than branded posts.**

**The Mebelchi sticker pack:**

- 24 stickers built around mebelchi humor: "Eman'ga $2/m² to'lash" with a sad face, "DXF tayyor" with a thumbs-up, "Brak yaratdik" with a facepalm, "Stiker yopishtirdik" with a green check
- Each pack is created with `@Stickers` (Telegram's native bot)
- Adding a single sticker to a chat carries the pack name in the metadata, which is forwarded with every use
- Every sticker spread = our brand spread, with zero effort and zero cost

This is the lowest-friction viral mechanism available. Even mebelchi who don't use our product will use the sticker pack because it's funny and culturally specific.

### 4.4 The forwardable cut-map PDF — the workflow-embedded virality

Per r1_4 hook #5: every cut map PDF the mebelchi sends to his customer or operator carries the Mebelchi logo at the bottom, a one-line "Built with Mebelchi (t.me/MebelchiBot?start=ref_[shop_id])", and a QR code that opens the bot with the referrer attribution baked in.

Customers see this. Operators see this. Other mebelchi the operator works for see this. **Every quote PDF is a free ad with attribution.**

### 4.5 Telegram's native Affiliate Program

Telegram launched native affiliate programs for bots in 2024/2025 — bots can host affiliate marketing programs with custom revenue sharing rates and variable commission periods. We do not need this in V1 (it's overkill for our scale), but it exists as an option for the supplier-partner side of the network. Year 2, when suppliers become affiliates, this might be the cleanest infrastructure.

## 5. Attribution architecture in a cash/Click/Payme market

The single hardest engineering problem of a referral program in our market: attribution. We cannot rely on URL parameters surviving from a Telegram chat through to a Click payment confirmation. Below is the architecture that works.

### 5.1 The attribution tree

The data model has three nodes:

```
Referrer (Telegram user_id, shop_id in our DB)
   │
   ├─ has unique deep link: t.me/MebelchiBot?start=ref_<encoded_shop_id>
   │
   ▼
Invitee event 1: Telegram bot /start with referrer param
   │ → log: referrer_id, invitee_telegram_id, timestamp, channel ("forward"/"link"/"voice")
   ▼
Invitee event 2: trial signup
   │ → log: invitee_shop_id created, referrer attribution preserved in shop record
   ▼
Invitee event 3: first paid invoice via Click or Payme
   │ → trigger: referrer credit (1 month free, queued)
   ▼
Invitee event 4: still paid at day 30
   │ → trigger: referrer credit applied (the "quality holdback" from r1_5)
   ▼
Invitee event 5: still paid at day 60
   │ → publish referrer's name on the public scoreboard
```

The attribution starts at the Telegram bot /start command — which is bulletproof because Telegram passes the start parameter natively. Once we have it, the attribution lives in our database as a foreign key on the shop record, and it survives every subsequent payment regardless of payment method (Click, Payme, cash, bank transfer).

### 5.2 The Click/Payme problem and the workaround

Click and Payme do not pass UTM parameters. They are payment processors, not attribution tools. **The workaround:** never attribute at payment time. Attribute at signup time (Telegram /start) and persist the referrer field on the shop record. By the time payment happens, the attribution is already locked.

This is the same architecture Romchi uses (per the CTO mentor) and the same architecture Procore uses internally.

### 5.3 Handling cash payments

For shops that pay via bank transfer with VAT invoice (per `09_QA_PLAYBOOK.md` Q5): the operator manually marks the invoice paid in the admin panel. The referrer attribution is still on the shop record, so the credit triggers automatically. The only edge case is in-person door-to-door cash collection (rare at $9/month — but possible). For those, the field rep marks the shop as paid via the operator app, attribution flows the same way.

### 5.4 Fraud mitigation at $9 ARPU

A motivated abuser could create fake mebelchi accounts to harvest 1-month-free credits. The mitigation layers:

1. **Telegram phone number required** for /start (Telegram OTP, per `03_PRODUCT_V1.md`)
2. **Shop verification within 30 days** — must add a real workshop address, must upload one photo of a real CNC or work area (auto-tagged), must complete one real cut list with non-trivial dimensions. The referrer credit only triggers after the referee passes verification.
3. **Cap referrals at 12/year** per referrer
4. **Manual review for referrers above 8 in a single year** — Oppoq or factory friend calls the top referrers to confirm legitimacy

This is the same model as Housecall Pro's "30-day quality holdback" from r1_5, calibrated for our price point.

### 5.5 The scoreboard publishing problem

For the public leaderboard to drive status, it must be public. But we cannot publish revenue data or shop financials. **What we publish:**

```
TARBIYACHILAR LIDERLAR JADVALI / РЕЙТИНГ НАСТАВНИКОВ — MAY 2026

  Rank  Master                  Mahalla         Referrals  Badge
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  1     Karim usta (Karimov K.) Sergeli         11         Bosh Usta ⭐⭐⭐
  2     Bahodir aka (Yusupov B) Yangiobod       9          Usta ⭐⭐
  3     Sherzod (Tursunov Sh.)  Korzinka mebel  7          Usta ⭐⭐
  4     Davron-aka              Samarkand       6          Usta ⭐⭐
  5     ...
```

Posted weekly in the public Mebelchi Telegram channel. Each name links to that master's "Find a Master" public profile (per r1_6). Only counts are public; revenue is not. This is the Salesforce Trailhead model adapted to Uzbek apprenticeship terminology.

- **% of paying customers who have referred at least once**: target ≥ 20%
- **Trial → paid conversion from referrals**: target ≥ 45%
- **Referrer retention vs non-referrer retention** (D60): target +10pp

The decision matrix:

- If K ≥ 0.4: double down — invest in custom Mini App for the referral flow, expand supplier discount offerings, run city-by-city Mebelchi Kuni events
- If K = 0.2–0.4: keep program running, focus on improving conversion per invite (voice notes, sticker propagation)
- If K < 0.2: program is broken — investigate whether the trust transfer is the issue (likely) vs the incentive structure (less likely). Most plausible fix: switch from individual referrer status to mahalla-team status (group ranks instead of individual ranks).

## 7. What I'm NOT recommending, and why

Several patterns from r1_4 and elsewhere look attractive but should be ruled out:

- ❌ **A standalone "refer.mebelchi.uz" website.** Our user does not visit websites. Telegram bot + in-app is sufficient.
- ❌ **Email referral campaigns.** Mebelchi don't read email. Wastes engineering time.
- ❌ **A leaderboard inside the Telegram channel with vote/like mechanics.** Telegram has no native voting. Don't fake it.
- ❌ **Tiered cash referral payouts ($10 → $20 → $50 by tier).** The awkwardness problem above. One free month is the only sustainable cash-equivalent.
- ❌ **A separate referral product feature (like Jobber's paid Referrals add-on).** That's a future product, not a V1 acquisition mechanism. Keep V1 focused on cutting/sticker/CNC.
- ❌ **TikTok or Instagram social proof loops.** Wrong platform for this audience. Telegram only.
- ❌ **A QR code on every sticker that, when scanned, opens the bot with the panel's referrer attribution.** Tempting because it would create an industry-first viral mechanism — but the panel sticker is consumed by the operator, not the customer, and adds attribution noise (was the new shop referred by the original mebelchi or by the operator?). Defer to V2.

## 8. The one-sentence summary

> Viral B2B SaaS in blue-collar trade clusters is not about coupons or hockey-stick K-factors — it is about engineering the natural Telegram forward + the natural in-mahalla conversation + the natural workshop-wall display so that every existing customer's day-to-day behavior produces measurable attribution and a status reward without cash ever changing hands.

Build the deep-link attribution layer in weeks 1–2. Onboard 20 founding tarbiyachi in week 3. Publish the first scoreboard in week 4. Hit K = 0.4 by day 60 or learn why not. Every line of code, every event, every certificate, every sticker is a forwardable artifact carrying the next mebelchi's referrer ID.
