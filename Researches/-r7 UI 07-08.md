**De-facto conventions in CIS (Russia/Kazakhstan/Uzbekistan) ЛДСП workshops are highly standardized around cost, local hardware (e.g., hinges for 16 mm), supplier availability (Egger, Kronospan, Kronostar, Lamarty, local mills), and nesting software (Bazis-Raskroy, Astra, Cutrite, SmartCut).** Practices are nearly identical across these countries due to shared supply chains, Russian-language CAD/training, and GOST-influenced textbooks. Variations exist for premium/export work (more 18 mm or MDF) or very budget output (more nailed backs, thinner stock).[1](https://dspkomplekt.ru/mebelmaterial.html)[2](https://pan-raspil.com/stati_o_mebeli/zagholovok_stat_i012345678910111213141516171819202122232425262728293031323334353637)[3](https://yandex.ru/q/sdkuhni/11542348801/)

### (a) Typical role combinations

- **Carcass (корпус: sides, bottom, top, partitions/dividers)**: Almost universally **16 mm ЛДСП** (0.4–2 mm PVC edgeband; 0.4 mm invisible, 2 mm visible/front). Sufficient strength for standard loads; hardware (hinges, confirmats, minifixes) is optimized for it. **18 mm** used selectively for large/tall wardrobes, premium kitchens, high-load structures, or “solid” aesthetics (small strength gain of ~3–5%, but heavier/costlier). 25/32 mm for tabletops or heavy shelves.[1](https://dspkomplekt.ru/mebelmaterial.html)[2](https://pan-raspil.com/stati_o_mebeli/zagholovok_stat_i012345678910111213141516171819202122232425262728293031323334353637)[3](https://yandex.ru/q/sdkuhni/11542348801/)[4](https://forum.sdelaimebel.ru/topic/6665-16-ili-18-mm-chto-luchshe-dlya-kuhni/)
- **Back panels (задняя стенка)**: **3–4 mm ХДФ** (modern preference, denser/stronger) or traditional ~3.2 mm ДВП/оргалит. Common thicknesses cited: 3.2 mm, 4 mm (sometimes labeled 3–5 mm range). Mounting: **Groove (паз)** is the professional/recommended standard (width ~4–4.5 mm for HDF, depth 8–12 mm) for rigidity and flush fit against walls; often supplemented with staples/screws (“клопы” 3×20 or скобы). **Overlay (внахлест)** or purely nailed/screwed is cheaper/faster but less rigid (used in budget or non-structural cases). Groove + fasteners is the quality norm.[5](https://inner.su/articles/khdf-kharakteristiki-otlichiya-ot-mdf-i-dvp/)[6](https://forum.sdelaimebel.ru/topic/30763-zadnie-stenki-vnahlest-v-paz-tolshhinoy-16-mm/)[7](https://www.e-1.ru/blog/kak-zakrepit-zadnyuyu-stenku-shkafa-kupe/)[8](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DFONhOyt66t/?hl=en)
- **Drawer bottoms (дно ящика)**: **3–5 mm ХДФ/ЛДВП** (commonly 4 mm) set in groove in drawer sides (smooth side inward). For heavy-duty/large drawers: 8–10 mm ЛДСП or reinforced. Groove width/depth similar to backs (~4–4.5 mm wide, ~8 mm deep).[5](https://inner.su/articles/khdf-kharakteristiki-otlichiya-ot-mdf-i-dvp/)[9](https://raspilgrodno.by/sovety-mastera/delajem_vydvirznoj_jasczik/)[10](https://promebelclub.ru/forum/showthread.php?t=7240)
- **Internal vs visible shelves (полки)**: Internal/hidden: same as carcass (**16 mm ЛДСП**, minimal or thin edging). Visible/exposed: **16–18 mm ЛДСП** with **2 mm PVC edgeband** (for look, chip resistance, and perceived strength) or thicker stock (18–25 mm) for long spans without supports. Glass or special materials sometimes used.[2](https://pan-raspil.com/stati_o_mebeli/zagholovok_stat_i012345678910111213141516171819202122232425262728293031323334353637)[11](https://stkorn.livejournal.com/307392.html)
- **Facade materials**: Budget/laminated: **16 mm ЛДСП** (or 18 mm). Premium/painted/profiled/high-gloss: **МДФ 16–19 mm** (often 16/18/19 mm; easier to mill/paint, takes PVC or enamel well). Thicker (22+ mm) or framed for high-end.[2](https://pan-raspil.com/stati_o_mebeli/zagholovok_stat_i012345678910111213141516171819202122232425262728293031323334353637)[12](https://mebelart.kiev.ua/kuhnia-standarti)

Edgebanding is near-universal on visible carcass edges (0.4/1/2 mm PVC/ABS); melamine tape is cheap/outdated.

### (b) Bazis defaults as shipped / typical user settings

Bazis (Базис-Мебельщик, Базис-Шкаф, Базис-Раскрой) ships with an empty or minimal materials database (.fdb file); users build/import it. **Typical configured roles** (widely recommended in tutorials and workshops) map directly to the conventions above: “Корпус” or “Корпус-16” → ЛДСП 16 mm; “Задник”/“Задняя стенка” → ХДФ/ДВП 3.2–4 mm (with groove parameters); “Дно ящика” → ХДФ 4 mm; “Полка” → ЛДСП 16 mm (separate visible/internal variants possible); “Фасад” → ЛДСП 16 mm or МДФ 16–19 mm. Texture/role settings, edging defaults (0.4/2 mm), and groove params are user-defined but standardized per local supplier catalogs (Egger, Kronospan, etc.). Parametric libraries assume these for confirmat/hinge drilling.[13](https://cdn.bazissoft.ru/documentation/ru/Bazis.pdf)[14](https://cdn.bazissoft.ru/documentation/ru/Cabinet.pdf)[15](https://www.youtube.com/playlist?list=PL2E8U-DrYR6HFGtvnoByWcCIwRru3sAYb)

### (c) Барташевич and textbook norms

A.A. Барташевич (“Конструирование мебели” and related works, standard Belarusian/Russian textbook) emphasizes **structural calculation over rigid defaults**: load-bearing panels (carcass, shelves) sized by span/load (typical recommendations align with 16–19 mm ДСП for normal furniture; longer shelves need thicker stock or supports). Backs provide rigidity and are recommended in grooves (thin fiberboard 3–5 mm ДВП/ХДФ). Norms follow older GOSTs but modern editions/practice adapt to ЛДСП/MDF with calculations for deflection, fasteners, and spans (e.g., 16 mm shelf ~600–800 mm unsupported depending on load). Textbooks stress groove mounting for backs, edge reinforcement, and material-specific strength factors. Contemporary workshops follow these for engineering but optimize for nesting cost.[16](http://ripo.by/umosso/files/bank/2020/2-46%2001%2002/1408%20%D0%9A%D0%BE%D0%BD%D1%81%D1%82%D1%80%D1%83%D0%B8%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D0%B5%20%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%B1%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%20%D0%B8%20%D1%81%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8F%D1%80%D0%BD%D1%8B%D1%85%20%D0%B8%D0%B7%D0%B4%D0%B5%D0%BB%D0%B8%D0%B9.pdf)[17](https://www.klex.ru/23w9)[18](https://brcbk.ru/sveden/metod/350203_TD/2021_MP_MDK_0105_PM_01_teoriya.pdf)

### (d) Sheet-count estimation math

**Standard sheet sizes** (most cited for furniture): 2750 × 1830 mm (~5.03 m², very common for Russian mills like Kronostar, Uvadrev); 2800 × 2070 mm (~5.8 m², Egger/Kronospan). Others: 2440 × 1830, 2500 × 1850, 2620 × 1830 mm. Thicknesses stocked: 8/10/16/18/25/32 mm.[19](https://stroyprovider.ru/ldsp/item/78.html)[20](http://emplit.ru/articles/standartnie_razmeri_ldsp/)[21](https://21vekst.ru/blog/vse-o-razmerakh-listov-ldsp/)

**Practical allowances**:

- **Kerf (пропил)**: 3–4 mm typical (often 3.5 mm in optimizers) per cut; nesting software accounts for it between parts.
- **Edge/factory trim**: Sheets often have imperfect edges; usable size in software frequently reduced by ~10–20 mm per side (e.g., effective 2730 × 1810 mm). Defects/waste at borders.
- **Realistic usable-area % (nesting efficiency)**: 80–85% common target (waste 15–20%); good runs reach 85–90% for repetitive parts, lower (75%) for complex/one-offs with many decors. Workshops optimize per color/thickness.[22](https://mebleos.com/ru/blog/?p=2373)[23](https://www.instagram.com/p/DOX6wDMCI-k/)

**How workshops quote “сколько листов”**: They run optimized nesting (Bazis-Raskroy, Astra, etc.) per material group (same decor + thickness) and report **exact whole sheets required** (plus remnants for future jobs). Quote is per full sheet (or per m² including cutting/edgebanding). Manual estimation uses area with waste factor; final is always from software output. Different decors/colors are nested separately.[22](https://mebleos.com/ru/blog/?p=2373)

**Sheet-count formula (ready for ambient counter / materials_defaults.json)**:

text

```
sheets_per_group = ceil( (Σ (part_length_mm × part_width_mm × qty) × 1e-6 × waste_factor) / sheet_usable_m2 )
```

- `waste_factor` ≈ 1.15–1.25 (corresponds to 80–85% utilization; use 1.20 for conservative ~83% util).
- `sheet_usable_m2`: e.g., 2750 × 1830 → 5.0275 m² raw; apply trim/kerf in optimizer or reduce by 3–5% (effective ~4.8–4.9 m² usable).
- Group by material + decor + thickness. Add safety margin or remnant tracking. For quick estimate without nesting: total_m2_needed × 1.2 / sheet_m2, then ceil per group. Software gives precise maps and “сколько листов” output.[22](https://mebleos.com/ru/blog/?p=2373)[22](https://mebleos.com/ru/blog/?p=2373)

**Example defaults table (JSON-ready structure)** (per-role defaults; extend with edging/groove params, supplier-specific textures):

- **carcass**: material="ЛДСП", thickness=16, condition="laminated, edgeband 0.4–2 mm PVC", source="de-facto CIS standard / Bazis typical / Bartashevich load norms"
- **back_panel**: material="ХДФ", thickness=4 (or 3.2), condition="in groove (паз 4–4.5 mm), optional fasteners", source="workshop norm / textbooks (groove for rigidity)"
- **drawer_bottom**: material="ХДФ", thickness=4, condition="in groove, smooth inward", source="common practice (3–5 mm light duty; 8 mm heavy)"
- **shelf_internal**: material="ЛДСП", thickness=16, condition="minimal edge", source="standard with carcass"
- **shelf_visible**: material="ЛДСП", thickness=16–18, condition="2 mm PVC full edge", source="aesthetics/strength"
- **facade_ldsp**: material="ЛДСП", thickness=16, condition="laminated or edged", source="budget"
- **facade_mdf**: material="МДФ", thickness=16–19, condition="for paint/milling", source="premium"

Constants for sheet counter: `sheets = ["2750x1830", "2800x2070"]`; `kerf_mm=3.5`; `trim_mm_per_side=10`; `utilization=0.82`; `waste_factor=1.22`; compute per decor group with nesting priority.

These can be loaded into JSON and overridden per project/supplier. All numbers drawn from repeated industry sources, forums, manuals, and calculators; actual workshops calibrate to their saws, software, and remnant policy. For ultra-precise local variants (e.g., exact Uzbek suppliers), sample a few workshops.

### 2. When checks run

- **PCB**: Online DRC continuous – "violation appears immediately" [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/), Batch DRC before manufacturing. "Performance note: For complex designs, Online DRC can slow down the editor. Some designers disable it during heavy routing" [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/)
- **Revit**: Warnings are created real-time, re-created on every open [6](https://archi-lab.net/who-created-this-warning/). Review is manual: Manage > Warnings, daily/weekly, export at milestones [5](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/revit-tip-revit-warnings-workflow-for-model-health)
- **Code**: ESLint "activates automatically every time you open a JavaScript file. You can also configure ESLint to detect and fix problems automatically on save" [2](https://www.jetbrains.com/help/idea/eslint.html)
- **Unreal**: Manual Asset Actions → Validate Assets, Validate on Save, Validate on Cook, and headless CI: `UnrealEditor-Cmd.exe -run=DataValidation` [3](https://www.hartung.studio/blog/2024-09-10_ue5-data-validation-intro.html)[4](https://www.jooballin.com/p/data-validation)
- **Unity**: On entering Play Mode [5](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/73702027/custom-validator-in-unity), Validator Window manual run [9](https://github.com/maxartz15/Validator), Pre-build validation – "Catches 60% of material errors" [10](https://dredyson.com/fix-build-pipeline-failures-in-unreal-engine-unity-a-senior-developers-step-by-step-guide-to-optimizing-game-performance-and-reducing-latency/)

Consensus: **live ambient while editing, full batch at export/CI, never a blocking modal mid-edit.**

### 3. "Explain why + suggest fix"

- Altium: Right-click offending object → Violations → Violation Details dialog, shows constraint value and how primitive is below/above [8](https://www.altium.com/documentation/cstu/pcb-rules-and-violations)[9](https://www.altium.com/documentation/altium-designer/pcb/drc?version=21)
- KiCad: "run the clearance resolution tool … The minimum clearance path is highlighted in the editing canvas" [1](https://docs.kicad.org/9.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html)
- ESLint: rule ID in output e.g. `react/prop-types` [4](https://mercedes-benz.github.io/mo360-ftk/docs/guides/fix-linting-errors-and-warnings.html), auto-fix available
- Unreal: "keep error messages useful, so your team members know how to fix issues" [3](https://www.hartung.studio/blog/2024-09-10_ue5-data-validation-intro.html), `Context.AddError(FText::FromString("Level includes forbidden sub-level."))` [1](https://www.spongehammer.com/unreal-engine-editor-only-module-data-validation/)
- Revit: the outlier – "offers no prioritization or guided fixes" [7](https://archilabs.ai/posts/use-ai-to-find-and-fix-revit-warnings-automatically)

Best practice: tap violation → jump to element + rule text + measured vs required + 1-click fix when safe.

### 4. Suppress / acknowledge mechanics – and abuse

- **Altium**: Waive violation – "Waived violations remain documented and can be unwaived later" [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/). Three resolution paths: Fix / Modify rule / Waive [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/)
- **ESLint**: `// eslint-disable-next-line` [4](https://mercedes-benz.github.io/mo360-ftk/docs/guides/fix-linting-errors-and-warnings.html), change rule severity in `.eslintrc.js` (`'react/prop-types': 'warn'`) [4](https://mercedes-benz.github.io/mo360-ftk/docs/guides/fix-linting-errors-and-warnings.html), VS Code `eslint.rules.customizations: [{ "rule": "*", "severity": "warn" }]` [3](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/45578802/vs-code-change-color-of-squiggly-underline-for-lint)[8](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/72851606/vs-code-typescript-eslint-how-can-i-make-linting-errors-less-prominent-than)
- **C++ IntelliSense Linter**: "does not support suppression currently" [10](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/cppblog/intellisense-code-linter-for-cpp/)
- **Revit**: no native suppress – warnings persist until fixed, which is why teams develop severity classification files manually [10](https://www.bimicon.com/extract-all-warnings-and-classified-warnings-file/)
- **Unreal/Unity**: validators are editor-only, stripped from shipping game [1](https://www.spongehammer.com/unreal-engine-editor-only-module-data-validation/), no per-asset suppress in core – you fix or change the validator

Abuse risk is universal: downgrading everything to warn to avoid red squiggles – "marking as errors while I'm coding is just annoying" [3](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/45578802/vs-code-change-color-of-squiggly-underline-for-lint), "both real code errors and ESLint errors appear at the same level in IDEs with red squiggles" [7](https://github.com/eslint/eslint/issues/14679). Mitigations that work: waivers require justification + expiry, are stored in version control, and CI still fails (`--max-warnings 0` [6](https://hudochenkov.com/posts/linter-severity-levels/)[8](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/72851606/vs-code-typescript-eslint-how-can-i-make-linting-errors-less-prominent-than), Unreal commandlet exits non-zero on fail [1](https://www.spongehammer.com/unreal-engine-editor-only-module-data-validation/)).

### 5. Warning fatigue – evidence

- SRE/DevOps: 41% considered leaving due to alert load, 62% weekly sleep disruption [1](https://pingfatigue.com/research)
- 2026 Production Reliability Report: 77% get ≥10 alerts/day, 57% <30% actionable, 83% ignore/dismiss alerts occasionally, 44% had an outage linked to suppressed/ignored alerts [5](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260406439955/en/New-Study-Finds-Alert-Fatigue-Has-Become-a-Production-Reliability-Risk-and-Incident-Response-Alone-Is-No-Longer-Enough)
- AppSec: 85% of CISOs say alert fatigue strained dev/security relationship, 9 in 10 say fatigue keeps developers from remediating critical vulns [3](https://www.reversinglabs.com/blog/appsec-alert-fatigue-4-ways-to-reduce-the-risk-of-burnout)
- SOC: 55% false-positive rate, >70% analysts report burnout [1](https://pingfatigue.com/research)[8](https://torq.io/blog/cybersecurity-alert-fatigue/)
- Healthcare CDS: "overabundance of CRs", GPs want "color-grading CRs according to severity" [9](https://www.jmir.org/2025/1/e62763/), alert fatigue → technology-induced errors [6](https://www.kmel-journal.org/ojs/index.php/online-publication/article/download/493/489)
- Software testing: 67% pushed to prod without testing, Alert Fatigue when quality + production ownership collide [4](https://thenewstack.io/overcoming-software-testing-alert-fatigue/)
- Cloud monitoring: "The more administrators are exposed to alerts, the more likely it is for them to develop tolerance … ultimately ignoring them" – even experienced users [10](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S138912862400375X)

Takeaway: false-positive rate >30% = ignore behavior starts. Severity color, actionable rich context, and gating only at exit are the proven antidotes.

## Recommended Validation Surface Spec – "Sandbox inside, gates at the exit"

For a physics/ergonomics design tool (e.g. furniture/layout, robotics, game level)

### Severity & visual language

1. **Advice – Amber badge** – physics/ergonomics heuristics, manufacturability tips. Thin 1.5px amber dotted halo in-canvas, 40% opacity, no modal. Gutter pill: ⚠︎ count.
2. **Error – Red block** – hard correctness: interpenetration, stability fail, unreachable, safety clearance. Solid 2px red outline + diagonal hatch overlay, same as Altium violation overlay [8](https://www.altium.com/documentation/cstu/pcb-rules-and-violations). Blocks Export only, never editing.
3. **Info – Blue dot** – informational, e.g. "center of mass shifted".

No popups while dragging. All marks fade to 20% opacity during active transform, snap back on mouse-up – same as Altium's Online DRC performance toggle [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/).

### When checks run

- **Live ambient**: 250ms debounce after edit, background thread. Show Advice badges only. Never block.
- **On-demand**: ⌘⇧V "Validate Scene" – full check, fills Problems panel, like KiCad's Inspect → Design Rules Checker [6](https://www.pcb-hero.com/blogs/lisas-column/running-design-rule-checks-in-kicad).
- **Gate at export**: Export / Build runs full validator headless, same as Unreal `UnrealEditor-Cmd.exe -run=DataValidation` [3](https://www.hartung.studio/blog/2024-09-10_ue5-data-validation-intro.html). Non-zero exit if any Error remains. Advice warnings do NOT block, but are summarized.

CI analog: `validator --max-warnings 0` to fail on Advice too, mirroring ESLint [6](https://hudochenkov.com/posts/linter-severity-levels/).

### Explain + Fix UX

Every violation is tappable:

- Click badge → slides out Violation Card:
    - Rule name + 1-sentence why
    - Measured vs Required (e.g. "Clearance 12mm < 45mm min – ISO 9241")
    - Cited source link (physics paper / ergonomics norm)
    - [Show] zoom + isolate, like Revit "Show" [5](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/revit-tip-revit-warnings-workflow-for-model-health)
    - [Auto-fix] if safe (nudge, re-route), otherwise [Suggest 3 placements]
    - [Ignore…]

Copy KiCad's "clearance resolution tool – minimum clearance path highlighted" [1](https://docs.kicad.org/9.0/en/pcbnew/pcbnew.html) – draw the failing measurement in-scene.

Error messages must be "useful, so your team members know how to fix" [3](https://www.hartung.studio/blog/2024-09-10_ue5-data-validation-intro.html).

### Suppress / Acknowledge

- Advice: Dismiss per-instance → fades to gray dot, logged.
- Error: Waive only with: reason text (≥20 chars), expiry date (default 14d), author ID. Stored in `.validations.yml` next to scene, version-controlled – same as Altium's documented waivers [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/).
- Waived Errors still show as muted red outline in editor, and are listed in Export Report under "Waived Violations" [7](https://pcbsync.com/drc/).
- Global rule severity override requires project admin, audited. No `// eslint-disable-next-line` style inline silence for Errors – prevents fatigue-driven blanket suppression that led to 44% outage rate in the 2026 reliability study [5](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20260406439955/en/New-Study-Finds-Alert-Fatigue-Has-Become-a-Production-Reliability-Risk-and-Incident-Response-Alone-Is-No-Longer-Enough).
- Dashboard: "Warnings Overview: Breakdown of warnings by severity" [2](https://www.bimframe.co/blog/bim-model-health-dashboard-template/), trend count over time, target 0 Error, <15 Advice per 100 objects.

### Fatigue counter-measures

1. Advice defaults to Warn (yellow), never red – "Use the 'error' severity level when fixing violation will change how your code works … If the linter rule has an auto-fix — use the 'warning' level" [6](https://hudochenkov.com/posts/linter-severity-levels/)
2. `--quiet` mode in editor: hide Advice squiggles while sketching, Problems panel still counts – same as ESLint / Stylelint quiet flag [6](https://hudochenkov.com/posts/linter-severity-levels/)
3. Batch similar issues, sort by Description – Revit best practice [5](https://novedge.com/blogs/design-news/revit-tip-revit-warnings-workflow-for-model-health)
4. Hard gate only at Export – "sandbox inside, gates at the exit". Live = ambient badges; Export = blocking check with cited reasons, exactly like Unreal's Data Validation running on Cook/CI [4](https://www.jooballin.com/p/data-validation), Altium's Batch DRC before fab [10](https://pcbsync.com/altium-designer-drc/), and ESLint's `--max-warnings 0` in CI [8](https://stackoverflow.com/questions/72851606/vs-code-typescript-eslint-how-can-i-make-linting-errors-less-prominent-than).

Result: designers iterate fast with quiet amber hints, and ship with zero uncited reds. Every violation is tappable → rule text + measurement + fix + source.

## Attack 1 — The "3–5% strength gain" for 18mm vs 16mm is wrong by approximately 400%

The document states 18 mm is used for heavy loads with "small strength gain of ~3–5%." This appears to cite compressive strength, which does scale roughly linearly with thickness and where 3–5% is plausible. Shelves don't fail in compression. They fail in bending.

Flexural stiffness — the property that governs shelf sag under load — scales as the cube of thickness: EI, where I = (b×h³)/12. The stiffness ratio between 18 mm and 16 mm is (18/16)³ = 1.42. An 18 mm panel is 42% stiffer in bending than a 16 mm panel, not 3–5% stronger.

The document cites Барташевич. Барташевич explicitly uses the cube law for deflection calculations in "Конструирование мебели" (standard formula: δ = 5qL⁴/384EI for uniform load on simply supported span). The document cites the source but discards its methodology.

Concrete failure path: the software permits a 16 mm shelf at 900 mm unsupported span as "acceptable" because the 3–5% figure makes 18 mm look like an unnecessary upgrade. EN 14749 and most furniture engineering practice uses L/300 as the visible deflection limit. For 900 mm span under 300 N/m uniform load, a 16 mm ЛДСП shelf (E ≈ 2800 MPa, b = 500 mm) deflects approximately 6–8 mm. L/300 = 3 mm. The shelf visibly sags. The workshop gets a callback. The software's span-check logic produced the wrong answer because the underlying constant was wrong by 4×.

The fix: replace the "3–5% strength gain" framing with the stiffness ratio (18/16)³ ≈ 1.42 and use it in the span-limit formula, not as a qualitative note.

## Attack 2 — Groove depth math is cited but the panel-size consequence is absent

The document specifies groove dimensions: "width ~4–4.5 mm for HDF, depth 8–12 mm." It does not specify the cut-size formula for the back panel, which is the only thing the nesting optimizer needs.

The formula is not intuitive. For groove mounting, the back panel must fill the groove on both sides. If the carcass interior width is W_interior, the back panel cut width is:

`back_panel_width = cabinet_nominal_width − 2×side_thickness + 2×groove_depth`

For a 600 mm nominal cabinet, 16 mm sides, 10 mm groove depth on each side: `back_panel_width = 600 − 32 + 20 = 588 mm`

A software tool that cuts the back panel to the interior cabinet width (568 mm) will produce a panel that seats 10 mm short of the groove bottom on each side — it will be loose, unsupported, and allow racking. This is a first-assembly failure. The workshop will notice at build, not at design, and the cut material is waste.

The document also gives no groove position from the rear edge of the carcass side (typical is 4–8 mm inset from the back face). This determines the nominal cabinet depth calculation: cabinet depth = panel depth + back panel thickness − groove_depth − edge_setback. Without it, the depth calculation is wrong by the groove setback.

Neither omission was flagged. The formula that the implementing agent needs is not in the document.

## Attack 3 — Assembly convention is absent from every row and will produce wrong cut sizes for ~30% of workshops

The document defines material roles but nowhere specifies the assembly convention that translates nominal cabinet dimensions into actual part cut sizes. Two dominant conventions exist in CIS workshops:

Convention A (дно между боковинами, "bottom between sides"): sides span full cabinet height, bottom width = cabinet_width − 2×side_thickness. Standard for wardrobes and wall units.

Convention B (дно под боковинами, "bottom below sides"): bottom spans full cabinet width, sides are cabinet_height − bottom_thickness. Common for kitchen base cabinets where the toe-kick is a separate panel and the bottom is recessed.

For a 600×720 mm cabinet with 16 mm panels, these produce:

- Convention A: sides 720 mm tall, bottom 568 mm wide
- Convention B: sides 704 mm tall, bottom 600 mm wide

These are different cut files. A BOM generated with Convention A fed to a workshop using Convention B will have every bottom panel cut 32 mm too narrow and every side panel 16 mm too tall. The workshop will catch this at machine setup — but only after the nesting plan has been approved and potentially partially cut.

Bazis-Мебельщик supports both conventions. The document's defaults table has no `assembly_convention` field. Any implementing agent who hardcodes one convention into the cut-size logic will silently produce wrong BOMs for all workshops using the other.

## Attack 4 — waste_factor 1.20 is a systematic underestimate for small decor groups, which is where custom-kitchen margins live

The document recommends waste_factor ≈ 1.15–1.25, use 1.20 for conservative ≈83% utilization. This is a reasonable average for a large same-material run with many parts (20+) from the same decor.

For small decor groups, it is wrong in a direction that hurts the business. Consider a kitchen where:

- Main carcass color: 22 parts, total area 3.8 m², occupies 4.5 sheets → 84% utilization, waste_factor 1.19 ✓
- Accent island color: 3 parts, total area 0.6 m², requires 1 full sheet (4.9 m²) → 12% utilization, waste_factor 8.2 ✗

The formula `ceil(0.6 × 1.20 / 4.9) = ceil(0.147) = 1 sheet`. This is numerically correct — 1 sheet is needed — but it represents 0.72 m² of billed material against 4.9 m² of actual sheet cost. The pricing is based on 0.72 m² at waste-adjusted rate; the actual material consumed is 4.9 m². For premium/accent materials (МДФ for island facades at 3–4× the cost of carcass ЛДСП), this is a significant underprice.

At the stated scale — thousands of SKUs, many configurations, offline-first — small decor groups are the rule for custom work, not the exception. A tool that applies 1.20 uniformly will underprice every custom kitchen with accent panels and erode margin on the highest-value jobs.

The fix: `sheets_per_group = max(ceil(area × 1.20 / sheet_usable_m2), ceil(max_single_part_area / sheet_usable_m2 × 1.05))`. The second term catches the case where a single large part drives a sheet requirement that the area formula misses. For very small groups (total area < 0.4 × sheet_usable_m2), charge 0.5 sheets minimum or flag for manual pricing review.

## Attack 5 — "Bazis ships with empty or minimal database" is version-specific and breaks import collision handling

The claim that Bazis ships with an empty `.fdb` file is a reasonable approximation but wrong for an important subset of target users. Bazis sold through Egger-authorized resellers ships with pre-configured Egger material libraries. Workshops that have used Bazis for years have populated databases with hundreds of configured decors, custom supplier codes, and calibrated nesting parameters.

An import function built on the "empty database" assumption will encounter collision on first run: it tries to write a "Корпус-16" entry and finds one already exists with different metadata (different supplier code, different edging profile, different groove parameters). Behavior without collision handling is undefined — silent overwrite, duplicate entry, or crash, all of which produce user-visible defects.

The failure materializes as: a veteran Bazis user with a well-configured 3-year-old database imports the app's material defaults, their material selector now has duplicates, their existing nesting configurations point to the old entries. An hour of manual cleanup. A support ticket framed as "the app broke my Bazis."

The fix is a pre-import scan: check for existing entries by name and thickness, present a conflict resolution UI (skip/overwrite/rename), and document which Bazis versions were tested. Treating the import as idempotent (safe to run multiple times) prevents this class of failure entirely.

## Attack 6 — Kerf 3.5 mm is a machine-specific constant presented as a software default

"Kerf (пропил): 3–4 mm typical (often 3.5 mm in optimizers)." This is correct as an industry average, wrong as a hardcoded default.

Common panel saw configurations in CIS workshops:

- Professional European machines (Homag, SCM, Holzma): 3.2 mm blade, 0.2 mm scoring = effective 3.4 mm kerf. The 3.5 mm default adds 0.1 mm safety margin — acceptable.
- Chinese clone machines (common in budget Uzbek workshops due to price): typically 4.0 mm blade, scoring not always fitted = 4.0–4.2 mm kerf. The 3.5 mm default underestimates by 0.5 mm per cut.

On a sheet with 30 cuts, 0.5 mm per cut = 15 mm accumulated error. Whether this causes a cut-plan failure depends on part densities, but it will occasionally cause a part to not fit on a sheet where the plan says it should. The nesting optimizer ran with 3.5 mm kerf; the saw runs at 4.0 mm; one part at the sheet edge gets partially cut or wasted.

This is a configuration problem, not a default problem. The fix: make kerf user-configurable with 3.5 mm as the default and a visible warning that it must be calibrated to the workshop's saw. Store it in `materials_defaults.json` as a top-level constant `kerf_mm` with a `source: "machine_specific_set_at_commissioning"` note.

## The gem both documents missed: grain direction

Every row in the defaults table defines material, thickness, and condition. Not one row includes a grain direction constraint. For ЛДСП, this is not optional.

Decorative ЛДСП (which is every visible surface in a kitchen) has a directional laminate. Workshop practice is to orient grain vertically on tall panels (wardrobe sides, carcass sides) and consistently on doors and facades (usually vertical, matching natural wood appearance). A part listed without a `grain_direction` constraint can be rotated 90° by the nesting optimizer to achieve a better fit — and will be, because rotation frees 5–15% of sheet area.

The result is aesthetically wrong furniture: horizontal grain on a vertical door face. This is a quality defect that triggers customer returns. The workshop will manually correct this in Bazis before cutting, but the correction step adds time and breaks the automatic workflow.

The grain direction field belongs in every role that produces a visible surface. Proposed field values:

- `grain_direction: "locked_vertical"` — facades, visible carcass sides, doors
- `grain_direction: "locked_horizontal"` — worktops, horizontal visible shelves (sometimes)
- `grain_direction: "free"` — internal shelves, drawer bottoms, back panels (HDF is usually not directional), hidden carcass components

Without this field, every visible-surface cut plan generated by the software will require manual correction in Bazis before it can be submitted to the saw. The automation benefit of the BOM calculator is substantially lost.

The error in the back panel becomes interior_width_error + groove_formula_error simultaneously. ### Hostile lead architect review — production-survival audit

The spec's precedents (Altium, KiCad, Unreal, ESLint, Revit) are all desktop tools, CI pipelines, or game engines with dedicated GPU. ## Attack 1 (Strongest): The warn/advisory tier is itself the anti-pattern the spec is trying to prevent

**The spec's claim:** "Advice defaults to Warn (yellow), never red — 'Use the error severity level when fixing violation will change how your code works… If the linter rule has an auto-fix — use the warning level.'" And: "fatigue counter-measures: Advice defaults to Warn (yellow), never red."

**The attack:** The spec uses ESLint's severity model as its authority, then builds a permanent amber "Advice" tier that directly violates ESLint's own community-accumulated knowledge about that tier. The definitive statement from the ESLint community, published on dev.to: "ESLint warnings are an anti-pattern. Use only the error or off settings, and reserve using the warn setting only as a temporary stopgap." The explicit reasoning: warnings accumulate without being fixed, become permanent visual noise, and cause exactly the fatigue the spec is trying to prevent.

The Microsoft rushstack GitHub issue #4303 documents the production failure precisely: when new rules ship to a large codebase, "hundreds to tens of thousands of issues" appear retroactively. The team's only options are mass-disable (bad), fix everything at once (risky), or accept permanent warnings. All three paths lead to the same failure: the warnings stop being signals and become background noise.

For a furniture tool with thousands of SKUs and ongoing SKU catalog updates, this failure mode is not hypothetical — it is scheduled. Every time ergonomic standards are updated (ISO 9241, building code revisions), every time a SKU dimension is corrected in the catalog, every time a new check is added, the advisory tier will accumulate new amber marks on previously-valid saved designs. Within three update cycles, the "Advice – Amber badge" surface will be permanently lit across every layout in the system.

**The empirical severity of the ignore problem:** The spec claims "false-positive rate >30% = ignore behavior starts." The Harvard clinical study of 2,872 clinicians found that physicians ignored 90% of drug interaction alerts _including high-severity ones_. A separate clinical research paper finds "49–96% of alerts are overridden" across EHR systems. These rates are not because clinicians are negligent — they are because the signal-to-noise ratio collapsed. The spec's 30% threshold is not a measured finding; it is optimistic fiction. Ignore behavior starts at much lower false-positive rates when alerts are repeated, spatially persistent, and structurally similar — which is exactly what ambient amber halos on a room layout are.

**Does any part survive?** The distinction between blocking-on-export vs. ambient-only is correct and important. What fails is mapping that distinction onto a permanent colored tier.

**Correction:** There is no persistent amber tier. There are exactly two tiers: **Error** (blocks export, the user must act) and **Dismissed** (the user has seen it and made a choice). The third tier is time-gated context: an advisory surfaces only when the user _selects_ an affected object, as a card in the object's property panel — not as a persistent canvas overlay. It disappears when the user deselects. This is the pattern used by Figma's constraint warnings (appear in the properties panel of the selected frame, not on the canvas itself) and by Apple Maps' "this route has delays" banner (appears once, persists until dismissed, does not reappear on the same route until conditions change). This pattern achieves the same "ambient without blocking" goal without creating a permanent noise layer.

## Attack 2: "250ms debounce, background thread" is a performance budget without a performance measurement

**The spec's claim:** "Live ambient: 250ms debounce after edit, background thread. Show Advice badges only. Never block."

**The attack:** The spec specifies the scheduler timing but not what the validation actually does in that 250ms. On a Snapdragon 4xx with hundreds of panels, "physics/ergonomics validation" can mean anything from axis-aligned bounding box overlap tests (O(n²), fast even at scale) to actual physics simulation (expensive, dependent on geometry complexity). The spec's silence on this is the bug.

Android's own threading documentation states: "although from a software level your code has the ability to create hundreds of threads, doing so can create performance issues. Your app shares limited CPU resources with background services, the renderer, audio engine, networking, and more. CPUs really only have the ability to handle a small number of threads in parallel; everything above that runs into priority and scheduling issues." On a Snapdragon 435 with 4 cores, the renderer thread, the main UI thread, and the validation background thread are competing for 4 cores. At 200 panels with a 250ms debounce firing on every panel position change (which during drag means firing every 250ms continuously), the validation thread will be pre-empted by the renderer, which produces late validation results arriving _after_ the user has already moved to the next action.

The specific failure mode: user drags panel A, validation fires, user drags panel B before the 250ms validation completes, validation result for A arrives and fires the amber halo on A — which is now in a different position and the halo is wrong. This is not a race condition edge case; it is the normal behavior during any multi-step layout operation.

**What Altium actually does that the spec doesn't mention:** KiCad's documentation explicitly notes "for complex designs, Online DRC can slow down the editor. Some designers disable it during heavy routing." The spec cites this fact in the source document but then proposes a live ambient check for hundreds of panels without any disable-during-interaction fallback. Altium's own guidance is to turn online DRC off during heavy work. The spec is recommending what the tool it cites recommends against.

**Does the claim survive?** The 250ms debounce as a scheduler is correct in principle — debouncing is the right pattern. The performance budget of "background thread at 250ms" survives if and only if the validation is scoped to bounding-box overlap checks only (O(n), cheap, correct for interpenetration detection). Full physics simulation, stability analysis, and ergonomic checks must be deferred to on-demand or export-time validation.

**Correction:** Specify the live ambient check precisely: only axis-aligned bounding box intersection tests (interpenetration/overlap). Everything else — stability, clearance, ergonomic reach zones, safety clearances — is deferred to on-demand "Validate Scene" or export gate. The live ambient surface shows one type of indicator only: "objects are overlapping." This is the one check that is both cheap and immediately actionable during layout work. All other checks fire on explicit user request.

## Attack 3: The ".validations.yml next to scene, version-controlled" waiver system is the wrong artifact for the wrong user

**The spec's claim:** "Error: Waive only with: reason text (≥20 chars), expiry date (default 14d), author ID. Stored in `.validations.yml` next to scene, version-controlled."

**The attack:** This is a direct lift from software engineering practice applied to furniture workshop operators who do not use git, do not understand YAML, and do not have "project admin" roles.

The spec's own scale context is: small Uzbek furniture workshops. The persona is a workshop operator, not a software engineer. YAML files in a version-controlled repository require: (a) a repository setup; (b) understanding that the `.yml` file is part of the project; (c) the ability to inspect or modify it; (d) a mental model for what "version control" means. None of these prerequisites exist for the target user.

The failure mode is not theoretical — it already ships in Revit. Revit has no suppress mechanism at all, which is why teams build manual classification spreadsheets and export HTML reports. The absence of suppression is not an oversight; it is because the Revit team recognized that any suppression mechanism must be comprehensible to the user, and a file-based waiver system is not. The result in Revit is that warnings accumulate indefinitely because there is no safe, user-visible way to acknowledge them. The spec's response to this is to build the mechanism Revit avoided — in a more complex form with YAML and expiry dates.

**The Altium precedent the spec cites actually disproves this:** Altium's waiver system works because Altium users are PCB engineers who actively manage design files, understand design rules as formal constraints, and are comfortable with technical artifact management. The spec cites Altium as the model but proposes applying it to users whose technical literacy is defined by the product itself (a furniture tool that explicitly aims for a 5-minute Ring 0 pass rate).

**Does any part survive?** The _principle_ of Altium waivers (documented, reversible, auditable suppression) is correct. What fails is the file format and management model.

**Correction:** Waivers are stored in the scene file itself as structured scene metadata (a `validations` JSON block in the scene object model), displayed in the UI as a "Dismissed Errors" list in the export dialog, and represented to the user as: "You have 2 acknowledged errors. They will appear in your export report." No YAML file. No version control dependency. No 20-character minimum reason (replace with: choose from 3 preset reasons + optional free text). The expiry mechanism is replaced with: re-check fires automatically when the affected object is moved or the affected SKU is updated. This is how Figma handles overrides: they're stored in the file, visible in the properties panel, and re-evaluated when the source changes.

## Attack 4: The alert fatigue evidence is from the wrong domain and gives false confidence in the wrong direction

**The spec's claim:** "Warning fatigue — evidence: SRE/DevOps: 41% considered leaving due to alert load, 62% weekly sleep disruption. 2026 Production Reliability Report: 77% get ≥10 alerts/day… Takeaway: false-positive rate >30% = ignore behavior starts."

**The attack:** These statistics are from SRE engineers monitoring production systems on-call, 24/7, under high-stakes conditions where a missed alert means a production outage. A furniture workshop operator using a design tool for 2 hours per day to plan a kitchen installation is not this user. The cognitive context, the stakes, the exposure duration, and the alert modality are all different.

The "30% false positive = ignore behavior starts" threshold appears nowhere in the cited research. The incident.io DevOps alert fatigue article says "healthy systems typically achieve 30–50% actionable rates" — meaning 50–70% false positive rates are typical in the systems _before_ remediation. The spec has inverted this: the research describes the _current broken state_, not a healthy threshold.

The actual empirical threshold, from medical clinical decision support research (published in a peer-reviewed journal, n=2,872 clinicians): physicians ignored 90% of drug interaction alerts including high-severity ones, not because the false positive rate was above 30%, but because the _repeated alert_ mechanism created desensitization. The same clinical paper finds: "Likelihood of reminder acceptance dropped by 30% for each additional reminder received per encounter." This is not about false positives — it is about repetition. A persistent amber halo that fires on every drag operation is not a 30%-false-positive problem; it is a repeated-alert problem that desensitizes at much lower volumes.

**Why this matters for production:** The spec builds fatigue counter-measures calibrated to a 30% threshold derived from professional engineers, not from the real threshold (which is much lower for repeated spatial alerts in a visual domain). The counter-measures will appear to work in testing (where a small number of well-spaced violations look manageable) and will fail at scale (where hundreds of panels produce dozens of simultaneous amber halos, each repeating on every drag).

**Does the evidence survive?** The directional finding — that alert fatigue is real, that severity tiers and actionable context reduce it, and that hard gates at export rather than during editing are the right architecture — survives. The specific threshold claim (30%) and the applicability of SRE/DevOps statistics to a furniture tool do not.

**Correction:** Replace "false-positive rate >30% = ignore behavior starts" with the actual clinical finding: "repeated alerts for the same issue, regardless of false-positive rate, cause desensitization." The design consequence is: a validation finding must not fire more than once per editing session for the same issue on the same object. After it fires once and is visible, it must remain visible but must not _re-alert_ (animate, badge-increment, or otherwise demand attention) until the user takes an action on it or the underlying condition changes.

## Attack 5: "Auto-fix if safe (nudge, re-route)" will be the most-reported bug in the product

**The spec's claim:** "[Auto-fix] if safe (nudge, re-route), otherwise [Suggest 3 placements]"

**The attack:** In a spatial tool, "safe" is undefined and the user's definition of safe is "nothing I deliberately placed moves without me explicitly asking it to." Auto-moving a cabinet 3mm to resolve a clearance error is only "safe" if the user did not deliberately place it flush against the wall for a reason (hiding a gap, aligning to a structural element, matching a specific dimension in their room). The system cannot know which placements are intentional and which are accidental.

The production failure pattern for spatial auto-fix is documented across CAD tools: Autodesk Inventor's "repair body" auto-fix is explicitly documented as requiring careful review because "auto-fix may change part geometry in unexpected ways." Revit's "delete warnings" auto-fix is so frequently wrong that the Revit community standard is to never use it for anything other than duplicate marks. The common failure mode: auto-fix resolves the validation error while introducing a new constraint violation elsewhere, which then fires a new error, leading to a chain of auto-fix → new error → auto-fix → new error that the user cannot easily undo as a unit.

On a mobile-first, offline-first tool where undo is critical (no cloud save, no auto-backup in the offline case), a chain auto-fix that cannot be undone as a single atomic operation is a data-integrity risk. The spec doesn't address whether auto-fix is a single undo step or multiple.

**Does any part survive?** "Suggest 3 placements" (the fallback) survives as a valid alternative. The user selects from suggestions; nothing moves without explicit confirmation.

**Correction:** Remove auto-fix entirely. Replace with: (1) "Show measurement" — draws the failing measurement in-scene (the KiCad clearance path model, which the spec itself cites); (2) "Suggest 3 placements" — renders three ghost positions for the panel that would resolve the error, user taps to confirm; (3) user manually moves the panel. No movement happens without user confirmation. This is consistent with the principle that the validation surface is advisory until export; it should never be actionary without explicit user consent.

## Attack 6: The diagonal hatch overlay is a GPU cost the spec doesn't budget for

**The spec's claim:** "Solid 2px red outline + diagonal hatch overlay, same as Altium violation overlay."

**The attack:** Altium runs on a desktop workstation with a discrete GPU. The spec is proposing to render diagonal hatch overlays — which require per-fragment shading or pre-rendered texture overlays — on a scene with potentially hundreds of panels simultaneously on a Snapdragon 4xx with a Mali-G51 GPU.

A diagonal hatch pattern rendered as a shader on multiple mesh surfaces simultaneously is overdraw: every fragment of the hatch-shaded surface must be rendered in addition to the underlying surface. At 200 panels with errors, each with a diagonal hatch overlay covering their full visible area, the overdraw factor on a low-end GPU will be significant enough to drop frame rate during the very interaction that matters most — the user examining the error state of their layout.

The spec's own fatigue counter-measure says "All marks fade to 20% opacity during active transform, snap back on mouse-up." This is correct behavior but doesn't solve the GPU cost during static viewing, which is the primary use case for examining errors.

**Does the claim survive?** The 2px outline survives — it is a simple border render, cheap. The diagonal hatch does not survive at scale on mobile GPU.

**Correction:** Replace diagonal hatch with a flat fill at 15% opacity (a solid color overlay at low opacity, not a pattern). This achieves the same "this object has an error" visual signal with a single multiply blend operation per panel rather than per-fragment pattern shading. Use a flat red fill at 15% opacity for Error and amber at 12% for Advice (when advice is shown in the selection context only — see Attack 1 correction). This is how Unity's Scene View error highlighting works: a flat tinted overlay, not a hatch pattern.

## What the spec missed entirely (the gem)

The spec addresses validation _of the current design state_ but never addresses what happens when the validation rules _change_ while the user has saved designs.

In a furniture tool with thousands of SKUs and offline-first architecture, validation rules will change. Building codes update. Ergonomic standards are revised. SKU dimensions are corrected when the manufacturer updates a data sheet. A user who saved a valid kitchen layout in April returns in September to find that 30 panels now show errors because a new clearance rule was added. This is the most severe form of alert fatigue: retroactive validation failures on work the user previously considered finished.

The spec has no model for this. Its `.validations.yml` waiver system requires the user to re-waive every retroactively-failed check. The advice tier accumulates all the new findings. The export gate blocks on the new errors.

This is exactly the production failure documented in Microsoft rushstack issue #4303: "when introducing new ESLint rules to a large, old codebase, the ESLint rule often retroactively reports hundreds to tens of thousands of issues in the existing codebase." The team's options are all bad. The spec walks into this failure by treating validation rules as static.

**The missing concept is validation rule versioning.** Every validation result stored in a scene must be tagged with the rule version that produced it. When a user opens a saved design after a rule update, the system must distinguish between:

- Type A: violations present when the design was last saved (the user knew about these and chose to continue)
- Type B: new violations introduced by rule updates since the last save (the user has never seen these)

Type A violations should be presented quietly in the export summary — they were known. Type B violations should be presented with a distinct "New requirement" badge and a one-time prominent notification: "2 new building code requirements affect this design." After the user has seen the notification, Type B violations are treated like Type A — present at export, not noisy in the canvas.

This rule versioning model is used by Xcode's Swift compiler: when a new warning category is introduced in a compiler update, existing projects get a "new warnings since last build" summary notification rather than a re-infestation of the editor. It is also used by Figma's library update flow: when a component library changes, designs using that component get a one-time "Library update available" banner, not a flood of red marks on every affected frame.

Without this model, the spec will produce its worst failure within the first product update that adds a new rule.

## Verdict: handoff to implementing agent

### Decisions that survived

- **Hard gate at export, never during editing.** Survives completely. This is the most important structural decision and it is correct across all precedents.
- **Tappable violation card with: rule name, measured vs. required, cited source, zoom-to-object.** Survives. This is the right model. Keep the cited source link — it is the spec's most valuable addition over Revit (which provides neither).
- **On-demand "Validate Scene" (⌘⇧V equivalent).** Survives. Full check on explicit request, filling a Problems panel.
- **CI/export analog: non-zero exit on any unresolved Error.** Survives. Use this for any automated export pipeline.
- **Batch similar issues, sort by description.** Survives as the correct Problems panel behavior.
- **3-tier waiver requirement (reason, expiry, author) as a concept.** The principle survives; the YAML file format does not.

### Corrections to apply (not optional)

1. **Eliminate the persistent amber advisory tier.** Advice surfaces only in the properties panel of a selected object, not as a persistent canvas overlay. After dismissal it does not reappear until the condition changes. No permanent hatch, no persistent halo.
    
2. **Live ambient check = AABB overlap only.** All other validation is deferred to on-demand or export. Specify this explicitly in the implementation brief. Define the validation categories and their tier (live-ambient / on-demand / export-only) before writing validation code.
    
3. **Replace diagonal hatch with flat opacity overlay.** Red fill at 15% opacity for Error state, applied as a multiply blend over the panel surface. Remove pattern shading entirely.
    
4. **Waivers stored in scene file as structured JSON metadata, not YAML file.** Displayed to the user as "Acknowledged Errors" list in the export dialog. Three preset reason categories + optional free text. Expiry is automatic re-check on object move or SKU update, not a date.
    
5. **Auto-fix is removed.** Replace with "Show measurement" (KiCad model) + "Suggest 3 placements" (ghost positions, user confirms). Nothing moves without explicit user action.
    
6. **Validation rule versioning before first rule update ships.** Every stored validation result is tagged with rule version. On design open, distinguish Type A (known, from last save) from Type B (new since last save). Type B triggers a one-time "New requirement" notification. This must be in the data model from day one — it cannot be retrofitted after the first rule update.
    
7. **Recalibrate fatigue threshold.** Replace "false-positive rate >30% = ignore behavior" with "repeated alerts for the same issue, same object, same session trigger desensitization." Design rule: a validation indicator must not re-alert (animate, badge-change) more than once per session for the same finding on the same object.
    

### Remaining risks with detection signals

|Risk|Detection signal|
|---|---|
