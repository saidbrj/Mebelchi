## Phase 1: The Identity You Deploy

### You Are NOT a Founder. You Are a Practitioner.

This is the single most important rule of Telegram guerrilla infiltration. The moment you introduce yourself as "founder of [product name]," you are radioactive. Blue-collar operators have finely tuned corporate-smell detectors.

**Your operative identity must be:**

- A person genuinely interested in CNC efficiency and material optimization
- Someone who occasionally shares useful things they've built or found
- A contributor who asks real questions, not just answers them
- A persona with a backstory that is 100% defensible (never lie — just lead with your practitioner identity over your founder identity)

The sequence is: **Contributor → Trusted Voice → Helpful Tool Sharer → Product Advocate**

Never collapse this into a single step.

## Phase 3: The Drop Mechanism — How to Introduce It Without Being Killed

### The Rules of the Drop

**① Never post the tool cold into a group.** This is how you get banned in 4 minutes.

**② Always respond to a specific problem.** Wait for someone to post a complaint that your tool solves. Then respond to _their message specifically_ with your tool as the answer to _their problem._

Example trigger post you're waiting for:

> _"Cut three sheets of walnut today and had 30% offcuts I couldn't use. Anyone have a better way to plan this?"_

Your response:

> _"I built a rough calculator for exactly this — it's not fancy but it helped me stop wasting material on my own jobs. Here's the link if it helps you [link]. Would love to know if it works for walnut grain orientation too."_

Notice what that response does:

- Frames you as a **fellow practitioner who built a tool for yourself**
- Is directly **answering their stated problem**
- Includes a genuine question back to them (not a sales pitch)
- Has **zero product language**

**③ Deploy the tool via Telegram Bot, not a landing page.** A landing page looks like a campaign. A Telegram bot feels like a tool.

[3](https://magnetto.com/blog/top-ways-to-promote-your-b2b-business-on-telegram) Telegram bots let you automate key parts of the customer journey — like lead capture, client onboarding, product demos, and support.

The bot flow:

```
User sends /start

   Drop your email and I'll send you trial access."
```

## Phase 4: The Psychology of Blue-Collar Sharing — Why They Forward Your Tool to Competitors

This is counterintuitive and crucial. Here are the psychological mechanisms that make shop operators share tools even with direct competitors:

### Hook #1: The "I Found Something Good" Identity Signal

In trade communities, being the person who **discovers and shares useful things** is a status signal. It says: _"I'm plugged in, I'm sharp, and I'm generous."_ Forwarding your tool to a competitor doesn't hurt them — it **elevates their reputation** in the group as the person who found it.

[4](https://blog.crescitaly.com/telegram-marketing-strategies-that-actually-work-in-2024/) Word-of-mouth is Telegram's rocket fuel. Since it lacks an internal content discovery engine, growth often comes through forwards, mentions in groups, and invite links.

### Hook #2: The Reciprocity Loop

Blue-collar trade communities run on reciprocal information exchange. When someone shares a useful tool, they are making a social deposit. They expect that deposit to be returned in the form of tips, referrals, or problem-solving help later. Your tool becomes **social currency**.

**Design explicitly for this:** Make the tool shareable by building in a "Share with your team" or "Send to a colleague" button. Frame it as: _"Built this for shops like yours — pass it on if it helps."_

### Hook #3: The Competitive Equalizer Framing

Shop operators don't fear competitors having a waste calculator. They fear competitors having **secret advantages** they don't know about. If a tool exists that optimizes cutting plans, every operator wants it — not because they want to share it, but because they can't afford for a competitor to have it and them not to.

Frame your tool as: _"Large shops have been using software like this for years. This is the version built for independent shops."_ You've just activated the **underdog instinct**.

### Hook #4: Craft Pride and Professional Legitimacy

[5](https://thebigmarketing.com/telegram-marketing-strategy/) By consistently delivering high-quality content, you establish credibility and encourage word-of-mouth promotion as your subscribers share your content with their networks.

Woodworkers and furniture makers have enormous craft pride. A tool that makes their work **more precise, less wasteful, and more professional** doesn't just save money — it aligns with their identity as skilled craftspeople. They share it because it reflects their values.

**Build this into your messaging:** Don't say "save money." Say "cut cleaner, waste less, work smarter." The emotional register is entirely different.

### Hook #5: The "Post Your Results" Viral Loop

Build a sharing mechanism directly into the tool output. When the bot delivers an optimized cutting plan, include:

> _"This layout saves you 2.3 sheets vs. manual planning. Screenshot it and show the crew — or drop it in the group if you want feedback on the cut sequence."_

You've just asked them to **publicly display your tool's output** in the same Telegram group you're trying to penetrate. Every screenshot is an organic ad.

## Phase 5: Lead Capture and Qualification

### The PQL Pipeline (Product-Qualified Leads)

[12](https://www.gravitatedesign.com/blog/b2b-saas-lead-generation-guide/) A Product Qualified Lead (PQL) is a user who has experienced real value from your SaaS product—often through a free trial or freemium model—and demonstrates buying intent. PQLs typically convert at significantly higher rates than other lead types. Unlike MQLs who've just shown interest, PQLs have used your product and proven they understand its value.

Your Telegram bot is a PQL machine. Every person who completes a full optimization run in the free tool has self-identified as:

- A CNC operator ✓
- Actively running jobs ✓
- Motivated enough to input real data ✓
- Having experienced your product's core value ✓

This is a **far warmer lead** than anyone who clicked a Facebook ad.

### The Email Gate: When and How

The fatal mistake is gating too early. Let them use the tool **completely free at least once** before asking for anything. The sequence:

text

```
First use → No gate. Full value. Zero friction.
Second use → "Save your cutting plan? Drop your email."  
Third use → "Want multi-sheet optimization? Start your 14-day trial."
```

[2](https://7thgrowth.com/blog/the-underground-telegram-strategy-for-b2b-lead-gen/) The secret is to give value first and then sell.

### Nurture Sequence Post-Capture

Once you have their email, your sequence must speak their language:

- **Day 1:** "Your cutting plan is saved. Here's how to read the kerf allowances."
- **Day 3:** "Most shops using this cut material waste by 15–25%. Here's how they did it."
- **Day 7:** "Want to run multi-sheet jobs? Here's what that looks like for a 10-job week."
- **Day 14:** Direct outreach from a human: _"Hey, saw you've been using the optimizer — any questions about running it on your [machine type]?"_

## 4) Psychological hooks: why blue-collar operators will share a tool even with competitors

This is the part most SaaS founders misunderstand: sharing isn’t always “helping the enemy.” In tight trade communities, sharing can be rational and socially rewarded.

### Hook 1 — “I’m a pro; I measure my numbers”

Sharing a calculator signals competence. It’s a status move: _I run my shop by math, not vibes._

### Hook 2 — Reciprocity economy (“I share, you share”)

People share info with competitors when they expect future return—tips, supplier leads, machine settings, troubleshooting help. Research on sharing with competitors highlights that exchange relationships and expected reciprocity can make sharing rational. [4](https://insights.som.yale.edu/insights/when-should-companies-share-information-with-competitors)

### Hook 3 — The “argument weapon”

In trades groups, people don’t share tools to be nice—they share tools to **win debates**:

- “Your quote is off.”
- “That yield assumption is wrong.”
- “Kerf + grain direction changes this.”

A tool that produces a crisp number becomes ammunition.

### Hook 4 — Low-risk sharing (it doesn’t reveal _their_ secret sauce)

They’ll share utilities that don’t expose customer lists, pricing, or proprietary CAD libraries. A neutral calculator feels safe.

### Hook 5 — “Save the next guy from getting burned”

The most viral trades content is “avoid this expensive mistake.” If your utility is framed as mistake-prevention, it spreads.

### Hook 6 — “Raising the floor helps me”

When shops standardize on better quoting/yield math, it reduces underbidding and chaos. (Not everyone thinks this way, but the respected old hands often do.)

### Hook 7 — Identity: “this was built by a shop guy”

If you look like software people, they resist. If you look like an operator who built a wrench, they adopt.

### Hook 8 — Trust is source-dependent (and peer-driven)

B2B buyers lean heavily on trusted sources and peer networks; research on B2B e-WOM emphasizes longer cycles and risk reduction via recommendations. [5](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0148296323003156)

- **“Built by One of Us” Authenticity**: If your persona and bot voice are gritty, practical, and anti-BS (“No fancy AI bullshit, just what works on real 8x4 sheets”), it bypasses skepticism. Corporate tools get ignored; a hack from a fellow operator gets forwarded.
- **Curiosity + FOMO in Argumentative Groups**: These chats are full of debates (“Your nesting sucks”). A bot that objectively settles them spreads like wildfire. Once 3–4 members post results, the rest pile in to test it themselves.
- **Pragmatism Over Secrecy**: Most realize helping peers raises the whole trade’s game against bigger factories or cheap imports. It’s not giving away trade secrets—it’s a simple calculator.

Result: Organic spread. One seeded group can lead to invites in others. Expect 15–30% of active users to convert to lead capture when the free tier hits its natural limits on their real jobs.

### 4. Domination & Conversion Loop

- **Month 1–3**: Infiltrate, build reputation, seed bot in 5–8 groups. Collect early wins and testimonials.
- **Month 4–6**: Run challenges, create your owned group as central hub, cross-promote. Bot DM sequences nurture leads (“Want to turn these nestings into actual CNC files on your machine? Here’s the mobile tool we use in my shop”).
- **Scale**: Referral credits inside the bot (“Share this link, both get 20 extra free jobs”). Benchmark reports (“Guys in this cluster average 19% waste—top 10% are at 8%”) position you as the authority.
- **Metrics to watch**: Bot usage per group, signup rate on visual unlocks (~20–40% of heavy users), trial conversion from nurtured leads (target 15–25%).

**Cold truths and guardrails**: This takes 6–9 months of consistent presence to dominate clusters. If the bot sucks or feels salesy too early, you’re toast—deliver 80–90% value without signup. Test ruthlessly with real shop operators before wide release. Track everything anonymously at first.

This approach aligns perfectly with how these buyers actually decide: peer proof, tangible ROI on the shop floor, and tools that respect their time and language. Your LTV stays high because trust is earned in the same place they live. CAC approaches near-zero once the bot becomes community infrastructure.

Execute dirty, stay authentic, measure waste reduction you deliver. That’s the only marketing that works with this audience. The rest is noise.
